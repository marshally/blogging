# Writing Style: Will Larson

Will Larson is CTO at Imprint and has spent his career as a software engineering leader at Digg, Uber, Stripe, Calm, and Carta; he writes at lethain.com under the banner "Irrational Exuberance," a blog running continuously since 2007 that supplies the direct source material for his four books, *An Elegant Puzzle*, *Staff Engineer*, *The Engineering Executive's Primer*, and *Crafting Engineering Strategy*. His voice is instantly recognizable inside a genre — engineering-leadership writing — that otherwise trends toward motivational hustle-culture bravado or Twitter-native combativeness, because Larson is neither: he writes like a systems engineer who has simply redirected the same disposition (model the thing, name its parts, check the model against reality, revise) onto organizations and careers instead of infrastructure, and the calm, taxonomy-building, hedge-precisely register that results is what his readers actually mean when they call a piece of writing "very Will Larson." This corpus skews toward his management, strategy, and career writing, which is where his citation weight concentrates; it does not represent his systems-modeling work (the `systems` Python library, Jupyter-notebook models of hiring funnels and reliability) or his 2026 output on building internal AI agents, both of which would add a more technical, code-adjacent register this document doesn't capture.

## Corpus analyzed

1. [Staff engineer archetypes.](https://lethain.com/staff-engineer-archetypes/) (2020) — his flagship taxonomy; 367 points and 253 comments on Hacker News (2022 repost of the original), and the organizing frame that later expanded into staffeng.com and the *Staff Engineer* book.
2. [Hard to work with.](https://lethain.com/hard-to-work-with/) (2022) — 366 Hacker News points, 185 comments; his highest-engagement personal-vulnerability piece in this corpus.
3. [Writing an engineering strategy.](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/) (2023) — 305 Hacker News points, 51 comments; reprinted, by his own note in the piece, as "an unedited chapter" of *The Engineering Executive's Primer*.
4. [How to safely think in systems.](https://lethain.com/how-to-safely-think-in-systems/) (2021) — 188 Hacker News points; the clearest single statement of the systems-thinking register that runs under nearly everything else he writes.
5. [Measuring an engineering organization.](https://lethain.com/measuring-engineering-organizations/) (2023) — 141 Hacker News points, 69 comments; also reprinted as an *Engineering Executive's Primer* chapter.
6. [Work on what matters.](https://lethain.com/work-on-what-matters/) (2020) — 84 Hacker News points; a career-prioritization taxonomy (snacking, preening, chasing ghosts) that he keeps cross-linking from later pieces, including "Good engineering management" is a fad.
7. [Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt.](https://lethain.com/migrations/) (2018) — not independently charted on Hacker News, but the single most internally cross-referenced piece in this corpus: cited by name inside How to safely think in systems, Writing an engineering strategy ("the action is [conducting a migration]"), and Work on what matters, which is the clearest evidence of its canonical status within his own body of work.
8. [Setting engineering org values.](https://lethain.com/setting-engineering-org-values/) (2023) — also reprinted as an *Engineering Executive's Primer* chapter, and directly cross-linked from Writing an engineering strategy's discussion of policy "applicability."
9. ["Good engineering management" is a fad](https://lethain.com/good-eng-mgmt-is-a-fad/) (2025) — 218 Hacker News points, 118 comments; his most recent widely-discussed post, useful for confirming the voice has held steady across seven years of this corpus.
10. [Writers who operate.](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/) (2023) — featured on his own curated `/featured/` ("Popular") index; a rare reflexive piece in which he explains, in his own words, why he writes the way he does, which makes it unusually direct evidence for a style analysis.

## Who is writing

Larson writes as a practicing operator, not a consultant or a pundit: at the time each of these pieces went up he was running engineering at Uber, Stripe, Calm, or Carta, and he says as much explicitly — "Operating is, for me, remaining accountable for what I write. What I write is a pretty direct reflection of what I believe and how I operate at the time that I write it." ([Writers who operate](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/)) He treats the reader as a peer executive or senior engineer working through the same problem in real time rather than as a junior being instructed, so advice arrives as "here is what I did, here is the exact company and constraint it came from" rather than as universal law. He explicitly rejects two adjacent postures available to someone with his platform: the professional writer chasing distribution ("I've thought about this a lot, and have rejected that option because I believe that writers who operate...are best positioned to keep writing valuable work that advances the industry," [Writers who operate](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/)) and the commentator reacting to whatever is trending ("I don't believe you can say anything particularly novel or interesting in reaction to a trending topic," [Writers who operate](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/)). His relationship to the reader is collegial and mildly self-implicating rather than authoritative: he is as likely to cite his own failure as anyone else's, admitting he overworked until he "picked up the reputation for being difficult to manage" ([Hard to work with](https://lethain.com/hard-to-work-with/)), and credibility is built by naming the exact company, the exact internal value, and the exact outcome rather than gesturing at "some organizations."

## Core voice attributes

1. **Every abstraction gets tested against a specific, named example.** He almost never leaves a claim as pure theory. Describing how hard it is to write a value that actually works, he doesn't gesture at generic vagueness — he re-runs an actual dispute against Uber's actual stated value: "The product engineering manager disagreed. They argued that my concern violated the *Let Builders Build* value; living up to Uber's values was to approve the accelerated release, even if it risked significant user and business impact." ([Setting engineering org values](https://lethain.com/setting-engineering-org-values/)) The same instinct runs through the whole corpus: Amazon's *Dive Deep*, Stripe's *Seek feedback*, and Uber's *Meritocracy and Toe-Stepping* are named and tested rather than swapped out for a generic "Company X."
2. **The model-versus-reality discipline, applied to his own past decisions as readily as to anyone else's.** His clearest statement of first principles is also explicitly self-critical, and the worked example is a strategy he personally built and then had to abandon: "when your model and reality conflict, reality is always right... Attachment to our early model distracted us (too much time on collecting and classifying data) and we were slow to engage with the most important problems... We'd have been more impactful if we engaged directly with what reality was teaching us rather than looking for reasons to disregard reality's lessons." ([How to safely think in systems](https://lethain.com/how-to-safely-think-in-systems/))
3. **Layered, graduated hedges rather than a single softener.** Claims arrive with more than one qualifier stacked at different strengths, rather than one blanket disclaimer: "It's *possible* I would have improved more as a writer if I did it full-time, but I'm confident that I'm not a *meaningfully worse* writer due to the part-time nature of my writing. I also lightly hold the belief that I'm a better writer as a result of not writing full-time." ([Writers who operate](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/))
4. **Numbered or lettered taxonomies as the default mode of argument.** Rather than argue in continuous prose, he names a small number of discrete categories and walks through each with parallel structure: four Staff archetypes (Team Lead, Architect, Solver, Right Hand), three criteria for a useful value, four buckets of measurement. "A useful value is reversible, applicable, and honest: 1. **Reversible**: it can be rewritten to have a different or opposite perspective without being nonsensical..." ([Setting engineering org values](https://lethain.com/setting-engineering-org-values/))
5. **Restraint under provocation — the diagnostic register holds even when the situation is clearly frustrating.** Describing a CEO who won't hold an underperforming executive accountable, the prose stays clinical rather than indignant: "What I've come to appreciate is that the appropriate manager is almost always aware of the underlying issue, and for some reason they're simply unwilling to confront it." ([Hard to work with](https://lethain.com/hard-to-work-with/)) The one clear exception in this corpus — "If this is your approach to leadership, please know that you're awful" ([Work on what matters](https://lethain.com/work-on-what-matters/)) — registers precisely because it's rare enough to be the exception, not the rule.
6. **Personal admission used as evidence, not confession.** He discloses his own failures as a data point that supports the argument, not as a vulnerable aside seeking sympathy: "I'd like to say that I learned from my mistakes directly, but the honest version is that I came to understand this dynamic mostly through working with folks struggling from the same issue." ([Hard to work with](https://lethain.com/hard-to-work-with/))
7. **Intellectual debt is always named, immediately, at the point of use.** When a framework isn't his own, he says so and cites the source by name and book title rather than absorbing the idea silently: "Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* is the most approachable book on strategy that I've read, and I've learned a great deal from it." ([Writing an engineering strategy](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/)) The same habit opens How to safely think in systems, which ranks its two source books by name before making a single original claim.

## Structure and formatting

Titles are declarative and period-terminated — "Staff engineer archetypes.", "Hard to work with.", "Work on what matters." — an unusual convention for blog titles that makes nine of the ten pieces in this corpus read as a completed assertion rather than a headline hook; the lone exception, "Good engineering management" is a fad, drops the period because the title is already a quoted phrase. Openings are never a cold, generic scene-setter; they're anchored in one of three moves: a specific personal anecdote from his operating history (Migrations opens, "The most interesting migration I ever participated in was Uber's migration from Puppet-managed services to a fully self-service provisioning model..."), a named book or thinker he's synthesizing (How to safely think in systems opens by ranking the two most influential books he's read), or a reflective personal question ("Good engineering management" is a fad opens, "As I get older, I increasingly think about whether I'm spending my time the right way to advance my career and my life"). The rare exception that proves the rule is Writing an engineering strategy, which opens with an uncharacteristic bit of personification and onomatopoeia — "an invisible timer starts ticking in the background. Tick tick tick." — a device he doesn't repeat again anywhere else in this corpus. Longer pieces frequently state their own table of contents in bullet form before the argument begins, e.g. Writers who operate: "The themes I want to work through are: Evaluating believability for operators is much easier than for non-operators / The pursuit of distribution changes what and how authors write..." — he never withholds structure for suspense. Headers are sentence case and stated as topics or plain questions rather than clever hooks: "Which is right for you?", "Strong and weak team concepts", "Defining strategy", "Anti-patterns". Bold marks a term at the moment of its definition (**Team Lead**, **Diagnosis:**), italics carry that term through the rest of the piece once defined (*Team Lead*, *Architect*), and italics also mark book and publication titles. Numbered lists carry sequential process (the Derisk / Enable / Finish migration playbook, the ten-step strategy-writing process); bulleted lists carry parallel, non-sequential categories (the four archetypes, the anti-patterns, the values criteria). Definitional criteria are almost always followed immediately by a concrete **Example:** and often a **Counterexample:**, both drawn from named real companies rather than hypotheticals, as in the Reversible/Applicable/Honest treatment of Amazon's *Dive Deep* and Uber's *Be an Owner, not a Renter* in Setting engineering org values. When an abstract framework needs demonstrating at scale, he sometimes builds an entire invented-but-numerically-precise scenario rather than a one-line example — the "Example strategy" section of Writing an engineering strategy runs a whole fictional company's revenue splits, headcount ratios, and resourcing percentages ("Target 45% product engineering resourcing towards b2b, 35% towards consumers, and 10% towards new bets") before the general argument even starts. Diagrams are used sparingly and functionally, almost always simple relationship diagrams (three intersecting circles for business, product, and engineering strategy in Writing an engineering strategy) rather than decorative infographics. Internal hyperlink density is extremely high: nearly every paragraph links inline, mid-sentence, to one or more of his own earlier posts — "Only through [pacing your career to your life](https://lethain.com/forty-year-career/) can you sustain yourself for the long-term" ([Work on what matters](https://lethain.com/work-on-what-matters/)) — which turns each piece into a node in a cross-referenced personal wiki rather than a stand-alone essay; Migrations alone is cited by name inside three other pieces in this corpus. Pieces close without a triumphant flourish or an explicit call to action, typically restating the thesis in plain terms and often adding one more hedge as the very last word — Writers who operate ends "but you couldn't convince me that's not equally true for operators outside of–maybe–getting visibility into a small pool of direct competitors," qualifying its own final clause with a parenthetical "maybe." The 2018-era Migrations piece is the outlier: it closes by asking readers a direct question and thanking named collaborators by their Twitter handles ("Thanks to Ingrid, Jorge and Julia for shaping this post"), a convention absent from the later pieces in this corpus. When a post is a repurposed book chapter, he discloses it plainly rather than concealing it — "This is an unedited chapter from O'Reilly's *The Engineering Executive's Primer*" appears, set off by a horizontal rule, in three pieces here (Writing an engineering strategy, Measuring an engineering organization, Setting engineering org values).

## Reference palette

Systems and organizational theory supply his foundational vocabulary: Donella Meadows's *Thinking in Systems*, George Lakoff's *Don't Think of an Elephant*, and Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* (the explicit source of his Diagnosis / Guiding-policies / Coherent-actions structure), alongside Martin Fowler's writing on developer feedback loops and the SPACE framework and its predecessor, *Accelerate*. Named company leadership language is quoted rather than paraphrased throughout: Amazon's *Dive Deep*, *Customer Obsession*, *Are Right, A Lot*, and *Frugality*; Stripe's *Seek feedback* and *Be meticulous in your craft*; Uber's *Let Builders Build*, *Super Pumped*, *Be an Owner, not a Renter*, and *Meritocracy and Toe-Stepping*. His own operating history supplies the load-bearing anecdotes: Uber's Puppet-to-self-service-provisioning migration and its reliability and hiring-funnel modeling, Stripe's reliability-strategy model, Calm's founding "Engineering Strategy" document, and Digg's near-collapse (referenced as his personal experience of an "existential" company crisis in Work on what matters). Adjacent writers and practitioners are named and linked rather than gestured at: Charity Majors, Dan Na, Eugene Yan, Hunter Walk (the source of the "snacking" concept), and Tanya Reilly are all cited by name as fellow "writers who operate"; Cedric Chen's Commoncog essays supply his believability vocabulary, alongside Ray Dalio and Bridgewater directly. Economics and industry cycles appear as explanatory variables rather than color — zero-interest-rate policy (ZIRP), via Ranjan Roy's *ZIRP explains the world*, is used to explain why "good management" itself keeps changing definition. A small, deliberately restrained pop-culture register colors an archetype without ever carrying an argument: the *Right Hand* Staff-engineer archetype is compared to *Game of Thrones*'s Hand of the King and *The West Wing*'s Leo McGarry, and the team-lead figure from Tracy Kidder's *Soul of a New Machine* stands in for a manager whose style would read badly by today's standards but was correct for its era. Crowdsourced practitioner evidence functions as primary source alongside his own experience: named individuals from staffeng.com's interview series (Diana Pojar, Joy Ebertz, Nelson Elhage, Michelle Bu, Rick Boone, and others) support the archetype taxonomy the same way a named company value supports an org-values claim.

## Sentence-level habits

His default parenthetical-interruption mark is an en-dash, not an em-dash, and it is always closed with no surrounding spaces — a genuinely distinctive habit, verified directly against the site's source HTML across this corpus: "This document–often an implicit document that no one's ever quite written down–is your constitutional document for running engineering" ([Writing an engineering strategy](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/)) and "The most frequent topic that comes up is career management–what should I do next?" ([Measuring an engineering organization](https://lethain.com/measuring-engineering-organizations/)). Illustrative numbers are given false-precision specificity even in fully invented scenarios rather than round gestures — a hypothetical company gets "45% product engineering resourcing towards b2b, 35% towards consumers, and 10% towards new bets" ([Writing an engineering strategy](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/)) rather than "roughly half." Rhetorical questions function as section-organizing devices, answered structurally rather than stacked for emotional pressure: "What is the information that you need to operate the engineering organization effectively?" is immediately followed by a four-item numbered answer ([Measuring an engineering organization](https://lethain.com/measuring-engineering-organizations/)). Parentheticals carry a second, quieter qualification rather than a joke: "(If I could go back in time, I would move 'resolve conflict with curiosity' into our values rather than including it in our strategy, but it's what I wrote at the time.)" ([Writing an engineering strategy](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/)) A single, dry literary allusion occasionally appears unflagged, dropped into an otherwise plain sentence about metrics: "it's a truth universally acknowledged, that an engineering executive must be in want of more engineers to support measuring their existing list of 'must have' metrics" ([Measuring an engineering organization](https://lethain.com/measuring-engineering-organizations/)) — the Austen pastiche is never pointed at or explained. Terms introduced in italics function like variables and recur consistently once defined: *Team Lead*, *Architect*, *Solver*, and *Right Hand* reappear in italics throughout Staff engineer archetypes exactly the way a defined variable recurs in a proof. Long sentences accumulate qualifying clauses rather than piling up parallel examples for emotional effect — contrast this with a writer like Charity Majors's comma-chained lists of grievances: "Escalations are only slow if we (in tech spec review, or in the management team) are slow, and we should fix that by addressing latency rather than assuming process is inherently slow!" ([Writing an engineering strategy](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/))

## Signature moves

1. **The three/four-criteria taxonomy, each leg demonstrated against a named real company.** Define a concept as a small number of testable properties, then run each property against Amazon, Stripe, and Uber by name rather than a hypothetical. "A useful value is reversible, applicable, and honest... Amazon's *Dive Deep* asks leaders to engage with the details at hand... This is reversible... This is applicable... This is honest: most folks I've worked with from Amazon have embodied this mentality." ([Setting engineering org values](https://lethain.com/setting-engineering-org-values/))
2. **The numbered rule: abstract statement first, then his own operating failure as the worked example.** "1. When your model and reality conflict, reality is always right... At Stripe, we developed a model to guide our reliability strategy. The model was intuitively quite good, but its real-world results were mixed." ([How to safely think in systems](https://lethain.com/how-to-safely-think-in-systems/))
3. **The fully worked fictional case study with invented but precise numbers.** Rather than illustrate a framework with one clean sentence, he builds an entire pretend company's finances and staffing ratios and runs the whole argument through it before returning to the general principle — the "Example strategy" section of [Writing an engineering strategy](https://lethain.com/eng-strategies/) is a standalone several-hundred-word document ("We support three business lines (consumer, business to business, and new experiments). 80% of revenue comes from consumer, 20% from b2b, and new experiments is pre-revenue.") that exists purely as a worked example.
4. **The up-front, bulleted table of contents.** Before the argument itself starts, state exactly what's coming in list form, so the reader can navigate or skip ahead. "The themes I want to work through are: Evaluating believability for operators is much easier than for non-operators / The pursuit of distribution changes what and how authors write..." ([Writers who operate](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/))
5. **Dense, inline self-citation that turns each post into a wiki node.** He hyperlinks to his own back catalog mid-sentence, constantly, rather than treating an essay as a stand-alone unit — the concept of "migration" alone routes to [/migrations/](https://lethain.com/migrations/) from at least three separate posts in this corpus. "Only through [pacing your career to your life](https://lethain.com/forty-year-career/) can you sustain yourself for the long-term." ([Work on what matters](https://lethain.com/work-on-what-matters/))
6. **The declarative, period-terminated title.** Post titles read as completed assertions rather than headline hooks — "Staff engineer archetypes.", "Hard to work with.", "Work on what matters." — settling the calm, already-decided register before a single sentence of body text.

## What NOT to do (what Larson never does)

- Profanity is essentially absent: the one exception across this entire ten-piece corpus is a single mild "damn thing" in the 2018 Migrations post ("each team that endorses a migration is making a bet on you that you're going to get this damn thing done"), and nothing rises to that register again across the following seven years of writing represented here — a sharp contrast with the profanity-as-emphasis register of a writer like Charity Majors.
- Never uses ALL CAPS for vocal emphasis, and never uses emoji or kaomoji — none appear anywhere in this corpus.
- Never role-plays an advice-column persona or reproduces a reader's letter verbatim; even when the subject is deeply personal (his own burnout, his own "hard to work with" reputation), the address stays in measured first- or second-person prose rather than staged correspondence.
- Never opens with generic industry throat-clearing ("In today's fast-moving tech landscape..."); every opening in this corpus is anchored in a specific personal anecdote, a named book, or a direct personal question.
- Never anonymizes a company into "Company X" once he's ready to make the real point — Uber, Stripe, Amazon, and Calm are named, and their actual internal value language is quoted directly, even when the anecdote is unflattering (Uber's *Meritocracy and Toe-Stepping* value is presented as "a very accurate reflection of the actual internal culture" without softening).
- Never sustains a single extended metaphor or allegory across a whole piece — the "invisible timer... tick tick tick" personification that opens Writing an engineering strategy is dropped after one paragraph and never revisited; systems-thinking vocabulary (model, reality, leverage) stays literal for the rest of the corpus.
- Never issues a bare command without an attached rationale or an explicit escape hatch: "Don't do that. Wait." ([Setting engineering org values](https://lethain.com/setting-engineering-org-values/)) is immediately followed by "Wait at *least* six months. If that's too long, at minimum wait until you can evaluate whether a given value is honest or aspirational."
- Never reacts to a news-cycle controversy or writes a hot take, and says so explicitly rather than leaving it implicit: "I don't believe you can say anything particularly novel or interesting in reaction to a trending topic." ([Writers who operate](https://lethain.com/writers-who-operate/))
- Never claims a framework as uniquely his own without naming its source — Rumelt, Meadows, Lakoff, and McKinley are credited by name and book title at the exact point their ideas are used, not relegated to a footnote or omitted.
- Never ends a piece with a triumphant flourish or an explicit call-to-action; the last sentence typically restates the thesis in plain terms, sometimes adding one more qualifier rather than a rallying cry.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Against Charity Majors (charity.wtf), whose register is combative, profane at moments of highest conviction, and built out of her own re-embedded tweets, Larson is even-keeled throughout and treats his own past failures as data rather than material for a fight — where Majors tells a reader "Fuck em. Please leave," Larson's harshest line in this corpus is the comparatively clinical "please know that you're awful and take the time to work on yourself." Against Michael Lopp/Rands (*Managing Humans*, the Rands in Repose blog), whose voice leans on narrative scene-setting, pop-culture riffing, and emotionally expressive metaphor to make a management point land, Larson strips almost all of that away; the "invisible timer, tick tick tick" opening of Writing an engineering strategy is the rare exception to his default mode, and it disappears again within a paragraph. Against Camille Fournier (*The Manager's Path*), whose warmth reads as coaching and reassurance, Larson's care shows up as precision and hedged qualification rather than comfort — he would rather bound a claim correctly than make the reader feel better about it. What distinguishes Larson from all three is the taxonomy habit itself: where Majors, Lopp, and Fournier mostly argue in essay form, Larson defaults to naming a small number of discrete categories and walking through each one with parallel structure and a named real-world example, a method that reads like it comes from systems modeling rather than from essay-writing traditions.
