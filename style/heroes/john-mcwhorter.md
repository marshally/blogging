# Writing Style: John McWhorter

John McWhorter is a Columbia University linguist (his academic specialty is creole languages), a New York Times opinion writer, the host of the Lexicon Valley podcast, and the author of more than twenty books split between two beats: how language actually works (The Power of Babel, Words on the Move, Nine Nasty Words) and race in America (Woke Racism, Talking Back, Talking Black). His voice is distinctive because it is conversational erudition — a professor at the piano, playing dense material lightly: he teaches historical linguistics through burgers, lava lamps, underwear, and your own mouth; he delivers contrarian positions on race with a smile and an explicit concession to the other side; and he wraps donnish diction ("To wit," "as it were") around jokes a don isn't supposed to make. The corpus below mixes his most influential books with his most-cited essays and newsletter pieces, and every quotation in this document was verified verbatim against the source or a full repost of it.

## Who is writing

McWhorter writes as a working linguist who happens to find his field delightful rather than merely important, and who assumes you will too once he shows you the trick — his authority is disciplinary (he can gloss Greenlandic Eskimo and Proto-Germanic gender from memory) but his manner is that of an enthusiast at a dinner party, confiding rather than lecturing: "Don't tell the Scandinavians I said this, but 'Swedish,' 'Norwegian,' and 'Danish' are all really one 'language'" ([The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)). On race he writes as a black American liberal who considers himself pro-black and anti-racist while opposing what he sees as a performative orthodoxy, and he does it without rancor — he opens his most pointed newsletter essay by conceding his opponents' side of the question ("It isn't fair. I completely understand why they don't," [Kendi and DiAngelo Don't Debate People Like Me](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people)) and closes it by extending them the same courtesy he claims for himself ("I fully respect the same sentiment in others"). His relationship to the reader is direct, warm, and second-person — he addresses subscribers as "Readers and listeners:" and admits "Yes, I'm surprised too" when his own news surprises him ([Goodbye for Now](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/goodbye-for-now)) — and his most characteristic pedagogical move is to put the evidence in the reader's own mouth rather than in a citation: "Did you ever notice that when you learn a foreign language, one of the first things you have to unlearn as an English speaker is the way we use do in questions and in negative statements?" ([Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/3174939-our-magnificent-bastard-tongue-the-untold-story-of-english)).

## Core voice attributes

1. **Explains deep time through a homely object the reader already owns.** The hardest idea in his linguistics — that constant change is a language's natural state, not its decay — always arrives strapped to something domestic: "In fact, however, the most useful analogy to keep in mind is that a language is like a lava lamp. The 'lava' slowly swirls and clumps and rises and falls in its fluid in an eternal, mesmerizing flow. Although constantly changing, in no sense is the clump of lava decaying" ([Word on the Street](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/172499-word-on-the-street-debunking-the-myth-of-pure-standard-english)); euphemisms "require periodic replacement, like tyres," and "we'll change our terms just like we change our underwear, as a part of linguistic life in a civilised society" ([Euphemisms are like underwear](https://aeon.co/essays/euphemisms-are-like-underwear-best-changed-frequently), via [Languagehat](https://languagehat.com/mcwhorter-on-the-euphemism-treadmill/)); and Words on the Move opens on weather: "No one minds that today the clouds are neither in the same position nor in the same shapes they were yesterday. Yet more than a few mind that today the way people are talking is always changing" ([Words on the Move excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/323769271/Excerpt-From-Words-On-The-Move-By-John-McWhorter)).
2. **Plays the naughty don: formal scholarly diction colliding with bawdy or irreverent content.** The joke works because the syntax stays professorial while the material doesn't: "To wit, profanity first involved the holy, and only later the holes" ([Nine Nasty Words](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/86155165-nine-nasty-words-english-in-the-gutter-then-now-and-forever)); "English, however, is kinky. It has a predilection for dressing up like Welsh on lonely nights" ([Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/3174939-our-magnificent-bastard-tongue-the-untold-story-of-english)).
3. **Makes the reader perform the linguistics in their own mouth.** Rather than assert a sound change, he stages it as something you can catch yourself doing right now: "It's why someone who re-BELS is a REB-el, whose crimes you can re-CORD and thereby leave them on REC-ord for all to see" ([Words on the Move](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/48894416-words-on-the-move-why-english-won-t-and-can-t-sit-still-like-literall)); "People say 'nucular' modeled on other words that end in -ular such as spectacular, tubular, and vernacular" (same source); and even in the Times, on euphemism: "do you actually do much resting in a restroom?" ([How the N-Word Became Unsayable](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/04/30/opinion/john-mcwhorter-n-word-unsayable.html), via [Market Design](http://marketdesigner.blogspot.com/2021/05/repugnant-language-how-n-word-became.html)).
4. **Concedes the other side first, in short flat declaratives, before disagreeing.** His contrarianism opens with a handshake, not a swing: "It isn't fair. I completely understand why they don't." sits in the opening paragraph of an essay whose conclusion is a refusal ([Kendi and DiAngelo Don't Debate People Like Me](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people)); in Woke Racism he grants his opponents genuine value before objecting to their method: "We need the hard left to point us to new ways of thinking. However, we need them to go back to doing this while seated, with the rest of us, rather than standing up and getting their way by calling us moral perverts if we disagree with them and calling this speaking truth to power" ([Woke Racism](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/91122525-woke-racism-how-a-new-religion-has-betrayed-black-america)).
5. **Builds a long cumulative sentence that lands on a blunt colloquial punch.** The erudition piles up through commas and clauses, then detonates in plain speech, often with an exclamation point he genuinely means: "This cycle eloquently demonstrates that, in the end, dialects are all there is: the 'language' part is just politics!" ([The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)); "Where we have the space to carefully assemble and burnish our sentences, something like already or grocery store comes out; meanwhile, we spit out fuck when it's time to run away from a lion" ([Nine Nasty Words](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/86155165-nine-nasty-words-english-in-the-gutter-then-now-and-forever)).
6. **Credits other people's coinages scrupulously — and mints his own mischievously.** Pinker's term is always Pinker's: "What the cognitive psychologist and linguist Steven Pinker has artfully termed 'the euphemism treadmill' is not a tic or a stunt" ([Euphemisms are like underwear](https://aeon.co/essays/euphemisms-are-like-underwear-best-changed-frequently), via [Languagehat](https://languagehat.com/mcwhorter-on-the-euphemism-treadmill/)). Meanwhile his own coinages are deliberately playful portmanteaus a straight academic would never risk: "Social history is too complex to yield to the either/or gestures of KenDiAngelonian propositions" ([Woke Racism](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/91122525-woke-racism-how-a-new-religion-has-betrayed-black-america)).
7. **Defends the stigmatized variety by demonstrating its complexity, not by pleading for tolerance.** His argument for Black English is technical, not sentimental — the sympathy is earned through linguistics: "racism is hardly the only thing standing between how linguists see Black English and how the public sees it," and on code-switching, "The two things do not cancel each other out: They coexist" ([Talking Back, Talking Black](https://www.blpress.org/books/talking-back-talking-black/), via [Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/john-mcwhorter/talking-back-talking-black/)); the same instinct produces flat axioms like "there is no such thing as human beings speaking 'bad grammar'" ([The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)).
8. **Drops exotic data points whole, for spectacle as much as proof.** He trusts the raw specimen to delight: "In Greenlandic Eskimo, 'I should stop drinking' is Iminngernaveersaartunngortussaavunga" ([The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)); "In the 500s CE, Pope Gregory commanded his subjects to say 'God bless you' when someone sneezed because it was often the first sign of being infected with plague" ([Nine Nasty Words](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/86155165-nine-nasty-words-english-in-the-gutter-then-now-and-forever)).

## Structure and formatting

Pieces open by planting a concrete puzzle or a scene, never a thesis statement in the abstract: Words on the Move opens on clouds nobody minds changing ([excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/323769271/Excerpt-From-Words-On-The-Move-By-John-McWhorter)); Doing Our Own Thing opens not with a thesis about oratory but with a resurrected specimen of it — Edward Everett's forgotten two-hour Gettysburg oration ("Standing beneath this serene sky, overlooking these broad fields now reposing from the labors of the waning year..."), quoted at length before Lincoln's famous two-minute address even comes up ([Doing Our Own Thing](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/288738/doing-our-own-thing-by-john-mcwhorter/), via the [Wordorigins review](https://www.wordorigins.org/harmless-drudge/book-review-john-mcwhorters-doing-our-own-thing)); the Atlantic N-word piece opens on one professor, Laurie Sheck, and lands the ironic hook in the second beat — "She's also spent the summer in trouble with her bosses for possibly being a racist" ([The Idea That Whites Can't Refer to the N-Word](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2019/08/whites-refer-to-the-n-word/596872/), via [3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2019/08/the-idea-that-whites-cant-refer-to-the-n-word.html)). The argument then advances by parade-of-specimens: a principle is stated once, plainly, and then illustrated three, four, five times across unrelated languages, centuries, and registers (German keys and Spanish keys; Pope Gregory and Cor blimey; crippled to handicapped to differently abled), so the reader is convinced by accumulation rather than by syllogism. Digressions are frequent, short, and flagged as treats rather than apologized for — a tease withheld for fun ("I'll tease you a little; first initial is R!" [Kendi and DiAngelo](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people)), a "Note:" aside dropped mid-chapter ("Note: There is no such thing as someone who speaks your language with a perfect accent but whose sentences are full of grammatical mistakes," [Talking Back, Talking Black](https://screvi.com/highlights/talking-back-talking-black-truths-about-america-s-lingua-franca/)). Endings are short, firm, first-person stands — no trailing qualification — and often carry a buried allusion for the reader who will catch it: "I shall not seek, and I will not accept, calls to 'debate' people who find me dismissible, and I fully respect the same sentiment in others" ([Kendi and DiAngelo](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people)) echoes Lyndon Johnson's 1968 renunciation speech without footnoting it. His Substack headlines ran in all caps ("KENDI AND DIANGELO DON'T DEBATE PEOPLE LIKE ME"), and his newsletter voice addresses the audience collectively and warmly ("Readers and listeners:" ... "Once again, THANK YOU!!" [Goodbye for Now](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/goodbye-for-now)).

## Reference palette

Historical linguistics and philology first and always — Proto-Germanic genders, Old English battered by Vikings, Latin niger and Spanish negro, Samuel Johnson's dictionary despairing of embalming the language, Greenlandic, Saramaccan, Welsh. Deep history treated as gossip: Pope Gregory and the plague, medieval English speakers turning Richard into Dick "because of something medieval English speakers found jocular that only proves that humor dates badly" ([Nine Nasty Words](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/86155165-nine-nasty-words-english-in-the-gutter-then-now-and-forever)), the Panic of 1893, Edward Everett at Gettysburg, Woodrow Wilson's League of Nations speeches. Everyday Americana as teaching material: cheeseburgers and veggie burgers, restrooms, grocery stores, keys, underwear, tires, lava lamps, clouds. Music as a parallel art he knows from the inside — a pianist and show-tune devotee, he treats American song as data, arguing that "For all but the sliver of poetry fans, over the past forty years popular song lyrics have been the nation's poetry" and that "poetry becomes more like tai chi than baseball" ([Doing Our Own Thing](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/172501-doing-our-own-thing-the-degradation-of-language-and-music-and-why-we-sh)). On the race beat, the palette is civil-rights history and its iconography — Baldwin's 1963 broadcast, Jefferson Davis and Bull Connor, the "old-school segregationist brought alive to walk through modern America" ([Woke Racism](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/91122525-woke-racism-how-a-new-religion-has-betrayed-black-america)) — plus the contemporary academy and media he is arguing with by name (Kendi, DiAngelo, the 1619 Project).

## Sentence-level habits

The default rhythm is a long, clause-stacking sentence that keeps its balance through commas, dashes, and semicolons, relieved at intervals by a very short one that lands the point — and he is unafraid of the sincere exclamation mark at the moment of payoff ("the 'language' part is just politics!" [The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)). He self-interrupts with dashes mid-thought, a spoken-word tic imported to the page — it is baked into his own book title, Words on the Move: Why English Won't—and Can't—Sit Still (Like, Literally) ([Macmillan](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250143785/wordsonthemove/)), and it carries his pivots in the newsletter: "Yes, I'm surprised too — but I'm going to do it" ([Goodbye for Now](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/goodbye-for-now)). Direct address and rhetorical questions to "you" do real argumentative work rather than decoration: "Did you ever notice that when you learn a foreign language..." ([Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/3174939-our-magnificent-bastard-tongue-the-untold-story-of-english)); "do you actually do much resting in a restroom?" ([How the N-Word Became Unsayable](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/04/30/opinion/john-mcwhorter-n-word-unsayable.html), via [Market Design](http://marketdesigner.blogspot.com/2021/05/repugnant-language-how-n-word-became.html)). Donnish connectives and hedges — "To wit," "as it were," "Note:" — sit inside otherwise breezy paragraphs, and the conspiratorial aside is a signature: "Don't tell the Scandinavians I said this, but..." ([The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)). Italicized specimen words are handled like objects on a lab bench — already, grocery store, burger, like, literally — and when a taboo word is itself the specimen he prints it whole, on the stated principle that referring to a word is not wielding it: Sheck "was evoking a word with one of the richest, nastiest, and most complex ranges of meaning in the English language" ([The Idea That Whites Can't Refer to the N-Word](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2019/08/whites-refer-to-the-n-word/596872/), via [3 Quarks Daily](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2019/08/the-idea-that-whites-cant-refer-to-the-n-word.html)). Aphoristic near-chiasmus supplies the quotable lines: "the holy, and only later the holes"; "it is easier to change language than to change thought" ([Euphemisms are like underwear](https://aeon.co/essays/euphemisms-are-like-underwear-best-changed-frequently), via [Freethought Blogs](https://freethoughtblogs.com/singham/2026/06/27/why-euphemisms-keep-changing/)).

## Signature moves

1. **The Time-Travel Witness.** Resurrect a specific historical observer and let their bewilderment or revulsion measure how much language or society has changed — the argument becomes a scene. "We talk about eating a nice burger, and Abraham Lincoln brought back to life would picture us trying to consume a staid, small-town German tradesman" ([Words on the Move](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/48894416-words-on-the-move-why-english-won-t-and-can-t-sit-still-like-literall)); "an old-school segregationist brought alive to walk through modern America even in the deepest South would find it hard not to turn to the side of the road and retch at what he saw" ([Woke Racism](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/91122525-woke-racism-how-a-new-religion-has-betrayed-black-america)).
2. **The Mouth Demonstration.** Prove the sound change by making the reader say it: "someone who re-BELS is a REB-el, whose crimes you can re-CORD and thereby leave them on REC-ord" ([Words on the Move](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/48894416-words-on-the-move-why-english-won-t-and-can-t-sit-still-like-literall)) — capitalized stress marks standing in for a phonetics lecture.
3. **The Homely Analogy for Deep Time.** Map a millennia-scale process onto a household object or routine — lava lamp, clouds, underwear, tires — so the scariest idea (nothing about language holds still) becomes cozy: "we should expect that the terms we introduce to help us kick off new ways of thinking will require periodic replacement, like tyres" ([Euphemisms are like underwear](https://aeon.co/essays/euphemisms-are-like-underwear-best-changed-frequently), via [Freethought Blogs](https://freethoughtblogs.com/singham/2026/06/27/why-euphemisms-keep-changing/)).
4. **The Concessive Open.** Open the most combative piece by granting the opposition's strongest point in two flat sentences — in the Kendi/DiAngelo essay they are the third and fourth sentences of the piece — so the eventual disagreement reads as reasoned rather than tribal: "It isn't fair. I completely understand why they don't." ([Kendi and DiAngelo Don't Debate People Like Me](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people))
5. **The Naughty-Don Collision.** Set the most formal available register directly against the least dignified content and let the gap do the comedy: "To wit, profanity first involved the holy, and only later the holes" ([Nine Nasty Words](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/86155165-nine-nasty-words-english-in-the-gutter-then-now-and-forever)); "English, however, is kinky. It has a predilection for dressing up like Welsh on lonely nights" ([Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/3174939-our-magnificent-bastard-tongue-the-untold-story-of-english)).
6. **The Allusive Sign-Off.** Close on a short declarative that quietly samples a famous utterance, unfootnoted, as a gift to the reader who catches it: "I shall not seek, and I will not accept, calls to 'debate' people who find me dismissible" ([Kendi and DiAngelo Don't Debate People Like Me](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people)), riding on LBJ's "I shall not seek, and I will not accept, the nomination of my party."

## What NOT to do (what McWhorter never does)

- Never scolds the reader's own usage — the corpus contains no "you're saying it wrong"; even his one lament (Doing Our Own Thing) targets a cultural drift, and Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue explicitly waves off the pedantry: "the time English speakers devote to steeling themselves against, and complaining about, things like Billy and me, singular they, and impact as a verb would be better spent attending to genuine matters of graceful oral and written expression" ([Our Magnificent Bastard Tongue](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/3174939-our-magnificent-bastard-tongue-the-untold-story-of-english)).
- Never frames language change as decline — change is always presented as the medium's nature ("a language is something that is, when it is actually something always becoming," [Words on the Move excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/323769271/Excerpt-From-Words-On-The-Move-By-John-McWhorter)); a bad imitation would smuggle in nostalgia for "proper English."
- Never euphemizes the word under discussion — no asterisks, no "the F-word" dodges when the word itself is the subject; the NYT printed the slur in full 34 times on his mention-versus-use principle. A bad imitation would flinch.
- Never attacks opponents' motives or persons — Kendi and DiAngelo are named and argued with, but the essay about them ends "I fully respect the same sentiment in others" ([Kendi and DiAngelo](https://johnmcwhorter.substack.com/p/kendi-and-diangelo-dont-debate-people)); there is no sneering, no sarcasm-as-argument.
- Never leans on statistics, studies, or charts — his evidence is specimens (words, sentences, historical scenes) the reader can inspect directly, not regression tables; a bad imitation would add "studies show."
- Never writes a jargon wall — technical terms arrive pre-translated through analogy or demonstration; he would rather spend a paragraph on a lava lamp than use "diachronic" unglossed.
- Never hedges a thesis into mush — conclusions are stated as flat axioms ("there is no such thing as human beings speaking 'bad grammar,'" [The Power of Babel](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/521812-the-power-of-babel-a-natural-history-of-language)), then defended; he does not open with "it's complicated."
- Never performs solemnity to signal seriousness — even the heaviest subjects (slurs, race, profanity) get wit; but the wit is never shock for its own sake — every joke is also doing explanatory work.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Compared with Steven Pinker, the other great language popularizer he most often cites, McWhorter argues from philology and the reader's own mouth rather than from cognitive science and data — Pinker builds a case, McWhorter gives a recital, warmer, jokier, and more first-person. Compared with Malcolm Gladwell, whose counterintuitive turns are assembled from other people's research, McWhorter's contrarianism comes from inside his own discipline, so the surprising claim ("dialects are all there is") is a professional judgment rather than a borrowed finding. On race, he is the temperamental inverse of Ta-Nehisi Coates: where Coates writes incandescent moral witness in a tragic key, McWhorter writes cool, cheerful analysis that insists on progress ("so resplendent over the past fifty years") and concedes to opponents before dissenting. And compared with his longtime sparring partner Glenn Loury, who prosecutes the same positions with visible heat, McWhorter is the smiling half of the act — the one who would rather charm you out of a belief than defeat you in holding it.
