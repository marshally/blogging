# Writing Style: Michael Lopp (Rands)

Michael Lopp, who publishes as Rands, is a Silicon Valley engineering-management veteran — Borland, Netscape, eight-plus years at Apple, four years at Palantir, two years at Pinterest, VP of Engineering at Slack, and a return to Apple as Senior Director of Engineering — who has written at Rands in Repose since April 2002 and compiled the blog's best material into three books, *Managing Humans* (2007), *Being Geek* (2010), and *The Art of Leadership: Small Things, Done Well* (2020).  The voice is distinctive because it treats management writing as fiction-workshop material rather than business-school framework: instead of citing research, Lopp invents recurring capitalized characters — Stables and Volatiles, the Old Guard and the New Guard, Incrementalists and Completionists, the nerd with N.A.D.D. — and lets a small, concrete, often domestic scene (a ping-pong room, a knotted string of Christmas lights, a hoodie) carry an argument's full weight before he ever states the lesson as a rule.  It reads like a novelist's eye for archetype fused to a manager's obsession with the exact mechanics of 1:1s, status reports, and the precise moment someone quietly decides to quit.

## Corpus analyzed

1. [The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/) (2007) — Lopp's own top "Rands-defining" pick for first-time readers on his site's official orientation page, which describes it as the piece that "explains nerds to the significant others of nerds" ([Don't Skip This](https://randsinrepose.com/dont-skip-this/)); it spawned the companion honorable-mention N.A.D.D. and is the single most widely cited Rands essay across engineering-culture blogs.
2. [Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/) (2011) — the workplace-facing companion to the Nerd Handbook, discussed on Hacker News ([item 2112579](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=2112579)) and the essay that gives "the Nerd Burden" and "the Two Highs" their canonical statement.
3. [Bored People Quit](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/bored-people-quit/) (2011) — the second of Lopp's own three self-selected "Rands-defining pieces" ([Don't Skip This](https://randsinrepose.com/dont-skip-this/)), shared widely on Twitter and Hacker News within days of publication and still the standard reference on retention-through-boredom.
4. [The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/) (2010) — the top pick in a widely circulated, community-curated list of recommended Rands posts, and separately reproduced in a curated *Managing Humans*-themed reading repo; the canonical taxonomy of what actually happens inside a 1:1.
5. [Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/) (2012) — resurfaced on Hacker News years after publication ([item 23947536](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=23947536)) and anchors the same curated *Managing Humans* reading repo of essential Rands posts; the clearest single example of the paired-archetype technique.
6. [The Old Guard](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-old-guard/) (2014) — discussed on Hacker News ([item 8458812](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=8458812)), the standard citation for how founding-team culture and process friction evolve once a company scales past Dunbar's Number.
7. [Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/) (2016) — by Lopp's own account in his year-end review, "by far the most popular piece in an otherwise quiet publishing year" ([Rands in Review, 2016](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/rands-in-review-2016/)); still sold as branded merchandise on the site nearly a decade later.
8. [The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/) (2011) — an explicit, named homage to Joel Spolsky's Joel Test, listed in the same curated reading repo of essential Rands posts, and the corpus's clearest example of the scored-checklist format.
9. [N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/) (2003) — an honorable mention on Lopp's own [Don't Skip This](https://randsinrepose.com/dont-skip-this/) orientation page; the coined term traveled widely enough that Lopp later wrote a follow-up, [In Defense of N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/in-defense-of-n/), and logged mainstream press pickup in [NYT on N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nyt-on-nadd/)
10. [Incrementalists & Completionists](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/incrementalists-completionists/) (2003) — an early, tightly built example of the archetype-pair technique, later revisited in the follow-up essay [Heinous](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/heinous/), and the standing reference point for his recurring realists-versus-dreamers framing.

## Who is writing

Lopp introduces himself on his own site with a flat, disarming line: "Welcome. I'm Rands. That's not my real name, but that hasn't stopped me from writing in these parts for a long time." ([Don't Skip This](https://randsinrepose.com/dont-skip-this/)) The pen name comes from his wife's maiden name, adopted originally as a chat-room handle, and the persona built around it is a practitioner's, not a guru's: a career spent oscillating between engineering and management at Borland, Netscape, Apple (twice, eight-plus years the first stretch), Palantir, Pinterest, and Slack, where he served as VP of Engineering.  He writes as someone who is, by his own repeated admission, one of the nerds he is describing — the childhood N.A.D.D. diagnosis, the hoodie confession, the McDonald's collapse after over-exercising are all offered as his own case studies, not composite illustrations — so his authority comes from being simultaneously the subject and the clinician of the behavior he catalogs.  His stance toward the reader shifts by essay: sometimes "you" is the manager who needs to be told what to do ("Your job in a 1:1 is to give the smallest voice a chance to be heard," [The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/)), sometimes "you" is the nerd's bewildered partner being coached through a foreign species ("Your nerd has control issues." [The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)), and sometimes "you" is invited to self-diagnose as the nerd itself ("If your count comes close, you're probably afflicted as well." [N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/)).  Across all three postures he is gentle rather than confrontational — the project is to explain people to each other, not to indict anyone — and he treats his own managerial failures (the missed shields-down moment, the ignored early sign of boredom) as the corpus's primary evidence rather than as a rhetorical flourish.

## Core voice attributes

1. **Coins a named, capitalized archetype and lets it govern the rest of the argument.** Rather than describing a behavior pattern in the abstract, Lopp gives it a proper noun early and then treats it as settled taxonomy for the rest of the piece — and often for essays years later. "The birth of 1.0 initiates a split of the development team into two groups: Stables and Volatiles." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)) The same move built "The Old Guard" and "The New Guard" ([The Old Guard](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-old-guard/)) and, in miniature, "Incrementalists are realists... Completionists are dreamers." ([Incrementalists & Completionists](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/incrementalists-completionists/))
2. **Addresses the reader indirectly, through a possessed third party.** Instead of speaking to "you" about yourself, whole essays are built on "your nerd" as the addressee's problem to manage, which lets Lopp describe his own psychology while the reader is cast as an outside observer. "Your nerd has control issues." ... "Your nerd has built himself a cave." ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)) Managing Nerds keeps the same construction in the workplace register: "Your nerd treasures consistency." ([Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/))
3. **Converts his own private habits into public evidence.** Where a peer might cite a study, Lopp cites himself — a specific personal tic disclosed as data rather than color. "I write better when I'm wearing a hoodie. There's something warm and cave-like about having my head surrounded — it gives me permission to ignore the world." ([Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/)) The origin of N.A.D.D. itself is his own mother's diagnosis of him as a child: "My mother first helped diagnose me with NADD... I responded, 'Mom, I can't focus without all this noise.'" ([N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/))
4. **Builds the whole essay as the answer to a repeated pair of diagnostic questions.** The analytical skeleton of a piece frequently collapses into two paired questions, asked near-verbatim at the essay's start and echoed again near its close. "Where are they going?" and "What are you currently doing to get them there?" ([Bored People Quit](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/bored-people-quit/)) "Why are they leaving?" and "When did their shields go down?" ([Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/))
5. **Promotes a single declarative sentence to load-bearing status, distinct from the surrounding explanation.** The advice compresses into one flat sentence that functions as the essay's thesis. "If I had to give you a single piece of managerial advice, I would say: 'Your job with your nerd is to bring calm to their chaos'." ([Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/)) "Boredom is easier to fix than an absence of belief." ([Bored People Quit](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/bored-people-quit/))
6. **Renders unspoken internal monologue as quoted, italicized speech.** Rather than paraphrasing what someone is privately thinking, Lopp writes it as a direct quotation, indistinguishable in form from actual dialogue. "Whew, we did it." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)) "You know, I have no idea when I'm going to be a tech lead here." ([Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/))
7. **Escalates into ALL CAPS at the exact word or phrase carrying the emotional charge.** Capitalization marks either a suppressed emotion breaking through as imagined shouted dialogue, or a single word within an otherwise calm sentence that needs to land harder than the rest — never routine emphasis. "YOU THINK YOU WANT 1.0 BUT LOOK AT THESE SCARS – YOU DON'T WANT A PIECE OF THIS. IT'S WAR. WAR SUCKS." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)) "Their mantra is, 'If you're going to spend the time to solve a problem, solve it in a manner that you aren't going to be solving it AGAIN in three months.'" ([Incrementalists & Completionists](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/incrementalists-completionists/))
8. **Curses in his own narrating voice, sparingly, exactly at the point of highest conviction.** Unlike a writer who reserves profanity for quoted speech, Lopp swears directly as himself — but rarely enough that each instance still reads as emphasis rather than texture. "Nerds are fucking funny." ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)) "I'd sooner I admit I fucked up, because then I can figure out what I really did wrong faster, and that starts with someone saying 'No'." ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/))
9. **Names a peer's format explicitly and builds a homage on top of it rather than pretending to originality.** Where most of the corpus invents its own vocabulary from scratch, one piece is openly derivative by design, crediting the source before extending it. "It's hard to pick a single best work by Joel Spolsky, but if I was forced to, I'd pick The Joel Test... So it is completely an homage to Joel that I offer The Rands Test." ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/))

## Structure and formatting

Every piece on the site carries a short, lowercase, unpunctuated kicker line paired with its headline — a compressed dek that is frequently lifted verbatim from inside the essay itself, so the thesis is visible before a single paragraph is read: The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster carries the dek "a distinct lack of drama" — a phrase drawn directly from its own closing sentence, "Your reward for a culture of healthy 1:1s is a distinct lack of drama." ([The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/)); Shields Down carries the dek "Happy people don't leave jobs they love" — a sentence that also appears mid-essay verbatim, "Happy people don't leave jobs they love." ([Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/)); The Rands Test carries the dek "Start here" — echoing the essay's own standalone line, "Start here." ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/)).  Openings favor one of three moves and almost never a rhetorical question or an academic framing of the problem: a concrete scene with a named, specific person already in motion ("Stephen was a hired gun at my first start-up." [Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)); a flat, short declarative dropped cold ("Business is noisy." — the entire opening line of [The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/)); or an existing, respected concept invoked and then set aside in favor of his own better one ("Dunbar's Number is a favorite blunt diagnosis for the pains that affect rapidly growing teams... but I think there is a more interesting emergent behavior during rapid growth," [The Old Guard](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-old-guard/)).  Arguments are staged by introducing two opposed camps, letting each camp's grievance against the other stand as equally legitimate ("Volatiles believe Stables are fat, lazy, and bureaucratic. They believe Stables have become 'The Man.' Meanwhile, Stables believe Volatiles hold nothing sacred and are doing whatever they please... Bad news: everyone is right." [Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)), and resolving not into a declared winner but into an instruction to invest in both.  Headers are almost never literal markup — an actual `<h2>` appears only at the biggest movement breaks (the Nerd Handbook's "Advanced Nerd Tweakage" and "The Next High") — while every minor section break is instead a complete, bolded, second-person declarative sentence functioning simultaneously as an in-text header and a transition: "Your nerd has control issues." ... "Your nerd has built himself a cave." ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)); "Your nerd treasures consistency." ... "Your nerd also treasures efficiency." ([Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/)).  Numbered and scored lists carry an essay's analytical payoff, most explicitly in The Rands Test's twelve-point, plus-one/minus-one scored checklist modeled directly on the Joel Test ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/)), and in The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster's nested sub-taxonomies — "Move #1 – Three Prepared Points... Move #2 – The Mini-Performance Review... Move #3 – My Current Disaster." — and within the Vent itself, "#1 It's Done... #2 It's a Rant... #3 It's a Disaster" ([The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/)).  Endings rarely land as a triumphant call to arms: they close on a phrase that echoes the piece's own kicker or repeats a key word for emphasis ("Every moment as a leader is an opportunity to either strengthen or weaken shields. Every single moment." [Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/)), on a fourth-wall wink that turns the lesson back on the reader ("maybe someone somewhere is writing an article about your particular quirks. Good news, she's probably reading it right now." [The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)), or on a wry, unresolved shrug rather than a resolution ("the generation that never knew a world without it... they're going to be annoying in their own unique way." [N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/)).

## Reference palette

His metaphors run overwhelmingly through computing internals repurposed as psychology — monospace typefaces and command-line interfaces used to explain a nerd's "control issues" ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)), "the context switch" installed as the master concept behind both multitasking and N.A.D.D. ([N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/)), and asides like "Grand Unification Theory? Yeah, a nerd invented that so he could sleep at night." ([Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/)) Nerd and geek culture supplies a steady stream of specific, named touchstones rather than generic "pop culture": Harry Turtledove's alternate-history novel *Guns of the South*, Douglas Coupland, *Calvin and Hobbes*, Asimov, and *Watchmen* are all named directly as the media diet of someone with N.A.D.D. ([N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/)).  Domestic and relationship imagery does the heavy lifting in the Nerd Handbook register — tangled Christmas-tree lights, the evening dinner debrief, the "Cave," the "hoodie" — turning abstract psychological claims into props anyone who has lived with someone would instantly recognize.  Company growth and internal culture shifts are consistently narrated as war: "It's a war. The small group is at war with conventional wisdom... but, most importantly, they are at war with themselves." ([The Old Guard](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-old-guard/)) "We were cleaning up the results of Stephen's Volatile engineering coup for years, but during that clean-up we went from zero customers to 30." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)) External validity checks occasionally borrow from finance rather than academia — "I think you should independently judge your company the same way that Wall Street does: your company is either growing or dying." ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/)) — and, unusually for this corpus, one other named writer is cited directly and admiringly rather than folded in anonymously: Joel Spolsky and the Joel Test, credited by name as the explicit model for The Rands Test ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/)).

## Sentence-level habits

Short, single-sentence paragraphs supply rhythmic punctuation after a long explanatory run — "Someone bled." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)), "Oh dear." ([The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/)), "Bullshit." ([Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/)) — each landing as its own paragraph rather than a clause inside a longer one.  The second-person addressee shifts by design across the corpus rather than staying fixed: sometimes "you" is the manager being instructed, sometimes "you" is the nerd's partner being coached, and sometimes "you" is invited to self-diagnose as the nerd — "If your count comes close, you're probably afflicted as well." ([N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/)) A colloquial refrain recurs across otherwise unrelated pieces as connective tissue: "what the hell is going on" anchors both a header inside one essay and a fragmented, panicked beat inside another — "Tell them what the hell is going on." ([Bored People Quit](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/bored-people-quit/)) "What. The hell. Just happened?" ([N.A.D.D.](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/nadd/)) Hypothetical or composite dialogue drops into prose as an unattributed direct quotation, with no "she said" — the reader is trusted to recognize it as a type rather than a transcript: "It's the best I can do, Rands. I've got 15 people working for me." ([The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/)) Taxonomic lists are introduced with a colon and broken into short, parallel declarative clauses, each closed with a period rather than a semicolon, so the list reads as a sequence of verdicts rather than a sentence fragment: "Stables are engineers who: Happily work with direction and appreciate that there appears to be a plan... Play nice with others because they value an efficiently-run team... Are known for their calm reliability." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/)) Paragraphs chain into the next bolded header through a trailing connective clause rather than a hard break — "This is one of the reasons that…" ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)), "This fits nicely with the fact that…" ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/)) — so the essay reads as one continuous argument even though it is visually segmented by bolded sentence-headers throughout.

## Signature moves

1. **The Archetype Pair.** Coin two capitalized, pluralized character types who are natural enemies, show that each side's complaint about the other is legitimate, then resolve the tension into "invest in both" rather than picking a winner. Example: "Stables are engineers who... Volatiles are the engineers who... these guys and gals hate — hate — each other." ([Stables and Volatiles](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/))
2. **The Nerd Address.** Write an entire piece in second person about "your nerd" — a stand-in that lets Lopp describe his own psychology in the third person while the actual reader is cast as an outside observer coaching a foreign species. Example: "Your nerd has built himself a cave... If you want to understand your nerd, stare long and hard at his Cave." ([The Nerd Handbook](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-nerd-handbook/))
3. **The Twin-Question Skeleton.** Collapse an essay's entire diagnostic apparatus into two short, paired questions, asked near-verbatim at the start and echoed again near the close, so the reader can hold the whole framework in two sentences. Example: "Why are they leaving? When did their shields go down?" ([Shields Down](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/shields-down/))
4. **The Scored Homage.** Take a named peer's coined test format wholesale, credit it explicitly, and rebuild it point-by-point with the same plus/minus scoring convention rather than inventing new structure just to appear original. Example: "So it is completely an homage to Joel that I offer The Rands Test," followed by a twelve-point checklist scored "(+1)" and "(-1)" line by line. ([The Rands Test](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-rands-test/))
5. **The Bolded Sentence-Header.** Replace a topic-label header with a complete, second-person declarative sentence, bolded, that both closes the prior thought and launches the next movement of the essay. Example: "Your nerd treasures consistency." immediately followed, several paragraphs later, by "Your nerd also treasures efficiency." ([Managing Nerds](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/managing-nerds/))
6. **The Echoing Kicker.** Pair the headline with a short, lowercase dek pulled verbatim from inside the essay — frequently its own closing line — so the thesis is legible before the piece is read and then lands again, recognizably, as the final beat. Example: the dek "a distinct lack of drama" is also the literal last sentence of the piece: "Your reward for a culture of healthy 1:1s is a distinct lack of drama." ([The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/))

## What NOT to do (what Lopp never does)

- Never leaves a coined archetype as a loose observation — Stables, Volatiles, the Old Guard, and N.A.D.D. are all given a concrete instruction for the leader by the essay's end, not left as clever labels with no attached action.
- Never cites a study, survey, or third-party research as evidence — proof is always his own managed teams, his own disclosed habits (the hoodie, the McDonald's collapse, the childhood N.A.D.D. diagnosis), or an invented-but-specific scene, never a citation.
- Never lets a personal anecdote stand alone as just a story — it is always generalized into a named, reusable rule by the end of the same section.
- Never ends on a triumphant, rousing note — closings resolve into a wry shrug, an echoed phrase, or a quiet admission rather than a call to arms.
- Never abandons second person for a detached "organizations should" register, even when the observation is about company-wide, systemic behavior.
- Never uses profanity as constant texture — it appears once or twice per piece at most, which is exactly why each instance still reads as emphasis rather than voice.
- Never opens with a rhetorical question or an academic framing of the problem — openings are a scene, a flat declarative, or an existing idea invoked only to be set aside.
- Never hedges the model with "it depends" once an archetype is coined — for the length of the essay, Stables are Stables and Volatiles are Volatiles, full stop.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Against Joel Spolsky, whom Lopp names and explicitly credits as the model for The Rands Test, the debt is real but the underlying authority differs: Spolsky proves a claim with market economics and a worked technical mechanism (TCP/IP, wages), while Lopp proves the same kind of claim with interior psychology — his own and his invented characters' — and never touches a wage figure or a market-share number anywhere in this corpus.  Against Charity Majors, who also curses in her own voice and writes from real managerial scar tissue, Lopp is gentler and more clinical: Majors litigates a specific bad practice in real time and picks a fight (Friday deploy freezes, toxic on-call culture), while Lopp builds a taxonomy of character types and diagnostic questions and resolves conflict into "invest in both" rather than declaring a villain.  Against systematizers like Camille Fournier or Will Larson, whose frameworks are built to be applied by someone else's judgment across many different organizations, Lopp's frameworks stay anchored to a small, named, recurring cast — Stables, Volatiles, the Old Guard, the nerd with N.A.D.D. — that reads more like a personal mythology carried across two decades of writing than a management textbook's general-purpose taxonomy.
