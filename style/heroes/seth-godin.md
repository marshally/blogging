# Writing Style: Seth Godin

Seth Godin is a Tufts-and-Stanford-trained marketer who founded the online-marketing pioneer Yoyodyne (sold to Yahoo in 1998), then turned almost entirely to writing and teaching — roughly twenty books (Permission Marketing, Purple Cow, The Dip, Linchpin, Tribes, This Is Marketing among them) and a blog, seths.blog, that has posted almost daily since 2002 and now runs past 9,000 entries.  The voice is distinctive because it inverts the normal relationship between length and authority: instead of building a case across pages of evidence, Godin compresses an entire argument into a handful of short paragraphs and lets a single aphorism carry the weight a footnote would carry for another writer.  There is no comments section on his blog — he has said engaging with every objection would change how he writes — so the form itself is a one-directional daily letter, not a conversation, and the prose is built to be complete and quotable inside two hundred words rather than to invite a reply.

## Corpus analyzed

1. [Don't Shave That Yak!](https://seths.blog/2005/03/dont_shave_that/) (blog, 2005) — ranked #1 on Godin's own self-curated [Top 100](https://seths.blog/top-100/) list and #3 on the site's traffic-ranked [All-Time Most Popular Posts](https://seths.blog/popular/); coined "yak shaving," a term that escaped into general tech vocabulary.
2. [Ode: How to Tell a Great Story](https://seths.blog/2006/04/ode_how_to_tell/) (blog, 2006) — #2 on Top 100, #4 on All-Time Popular; compresses the thesis of his book *All Marketers Are Liars* into one list-structured post.
3. [Permission Marketing](https://seths.blog/2008/01/permission-mark/) (blog, 2008) — on both lists; restates the thesis of his 1999 book of the same name in one definitional post, a self-quotation he echoes almost verbatim a decade later in *This Is Marketing*.
4. [Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/) (blog, 2010) — on both lists; the blog-length seed of *Linchpin*'s "resistance" chapters, published the same season the book went to press.
5. [Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked: Pick Yourself](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/) (blog, 2011) — #17 on Top 100; one of his most independently recirculated lines, quoted on Goodreads, podcasts, and career-advice sites for over a decade.
6. [Quality and Effort](https://seths.blog/2018/11/quality-and-effort/) (blog, 2018) — #1 on the site's traffic-ranked All-Time Most Popular list, evidence his reach hasn't dimmed with time.
7. [*Purple Cow*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/641604.Purple_Cow) (book, 2003) — his first fully marketing-focused book; coined "Purple Cow" and "remarkable" as durable industry shorthand for differentiation.
8. [*The Dip*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/19096852-the-dip) (book, 2007) — the canonical statement of "strategic quitting"; still excerpted by outlets like [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/excerpt-seth-godins-the-dip-on-the-benefit-of-strategic-quitting/) more than a decade after publication.
9. [*Linchpin*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7155145-linchpin) (book, 2010) — his most personal manifesto, introducing "the lizard brain," "the resistance," and "art" as durable vocabulary that recurs throughout the rest of the corpus.
10. [*This Is Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/40549476-this-is-marketing) (book, 2018) — his most recent full synthesis book; coined "the smallest viable market," a direct descendant of *Permission Marketing*'s 1999 thesis.

Beyond these ten anchors, this analysis also draws briefly on a wider sample read for rhythm and confirmation: the blog posts [How to Be Remarkable](https://seths.blog/2007/01/how_to_be_remar/) (2007), [First, Ten](https://seths.blog/2009/04/first-ten/) (2009), [The World's Worst Boss](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-worlds-worst-boss/) (2010), [The First Rule of Doing Work That Matters](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-first-rule-of-doing-work-that-matters/) (2010), [The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/) (2011), [Art Is What We Call…](https://seths.blog/2011/02/art-is-what-we-call/) (2011), [Are You Making Something?](https://seths.blog/2011/03/are-you-making-something/) (2011), and [Talker's Block](https://seths.blog/2011/09/talkers-block/) (2011), plus the book [*Tribes*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/3828382-tribes) (2008).

## Who is writing

Godin studied computer science and philosophy at Tufts, took an MBA at Stanford, worked briefly in software, then founded Yoyodyne in 1995 — one of the first online direct-marketing companies — and sold it to Yahoo in 1998, where he served as VP of direct marketing before leaving to write full time.  He has since published close to twenty books, nearly all bestsellers, and posted at seths.blog almost every day for over two decades: a former direct-marketing operator who became the genre's conscience, arguing that marketing is not manipulation but "the generous act of helping others become who they seek to become" (*This Is Marketing*).  His relationship to the reader is that of an impatient, encouraging teacher who assumes the reader is capable and stuck, not incapable and in need of persuasion — the "you" he addresses is always mid-decision, on the verge of shipping or hiding, never a passive audience being informed.  That persona is reinforced by a structural absence: seths.blog carries no comments section, so the blog reads as a one-directional daily letter rather than a conversation, and the "reply" happens instead in the trackbacks, shares, and reader posts it spawns elsewhere on the web.

## Core voice attributes

1. **Extreme brevity as the default unit, not a constraint.** Many posts run under 200 words; the length is the primary formal choice, not something worked around. "Don't Shave That Yak!" resolves an entire argument — a coined term, a vivid extended example, a moral, and a closing aphorism — in roughly 230 words, closing: "The minute you start walking down a path toward a yak shaving party, it's worth making a compromise. Doing it well now is much better than doing it perfectly later." ([Don't Shave That Yak!](https://seths.blog/2005/03/dont_shave_that/)) "Are You Making Something?" compresses a whole argument about digital distraction into a few short paragraphs before landing on four words: "Go, make something. We need it!" ([Are You Making Something?](https://seths.blog/2011/03/are-you-making-something/))
2. **Assertion in place of evidence.** He does not cite studies, name researchers, or hedge with "research shows" — claims are stated as flat, self-evident fact and left to stand on their own authority. "A great story is true." ([Ode: How to Tell a Great Story](https://seths.blog/2006/04/ode_how_to_tell/)) "The Dip is the long slog between starting and mastery." ([*The Dip*](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/excerpt-seth-godins-the-dip-on-the-benefit-of-strategic-quitting/)) "In a crowded marketplace, fitting in is a failure. In a busy marketplace, not standing out is the same as being invisible." ([*Purple Cow*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/641604.Purple_Cow)) "Marketing is the generous act of helping others become who they seek to become." ([*This Is Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/40549476-this-is-marketing)) No footnote, survey, or named authority accompanies any of these; the aphorism is offered as its own proof.
3. **A coined abstraction that recurs, unglossed, across the whole corpus.** He names a psychological or organizational pattern once, then reuses the term for the rest of his career as though the reader already owns the vocabulary. "The lizard brain is the source of the resistance" ([*Linchpin*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7155145-linchpin)) traces directly back to "Your lizard brain is here to stay, and your job is to figure out how to quiet it and ignore it" ([Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/)) — same term, same claim, blog post feeding book. "Yak shaving," "the Dip," "permission marketing," and "the smallest viable market" all work the same way: coined once, then treated as settled vocabulary from then on.
4. **Second-person address used as a challenge, not a comfort.** "You" confronts the reader with a decision already in progress; it rarely reassures. "That would be you. Even if you're not self-employed, your boss is you." ([The World's Worst Boss](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-worlds-worst-boss/)) "No one is going to pick you. Pick yourself." ([Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked: Pick Yourself](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/))
5. **Repetition-with-variation as a structural engine.** A short sentence template repeats with one word changed, building a list that reads like a chant rather than a set of bullet points. "Great stories make a promise." "Great stories are trusted." "Great stories are subtle." "Great stories don't contradict themselves." ([Ode: How to Tell a Great Story](https://seths.blog/2006/04/ode_how_to_tell/)) The same device works at the single-sentence level: "Art is hard. Selling is hard. Writing is hard. Making a difference is hard." ([The First Rule of Doing Work That Matters](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-first-rule-of-doing-work-that-matters/))
6. **The cold open, with no throat-clearing.** Posts begin already mid-argument or mid-scene — no "in today's world," no paragraph of scene-setting before the claim. "Amanda Hocking is making a million dollars a year publishing her own work to the Kindle. No publisher." ([Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/)) "That would be you." ([The World's Worst Boss](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-worlds-worst-boss/))
7. **The closing turn — a challenge, not a summary.** Endings rarely recap the argument; they pivot into a command or a question handed back to the reader. "Now you've seen the icon and you know its name. What are you going to do about it?" ([Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/)) "Simple doesn't always mean easy." ([The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/))
8. **Process collapsed into a numbered or two-step compression.** Advice that another writer would unfold across an essay gets reduced to a short numbered structure of imperative fragments. "Step one: Open all doors. Learn a little about a lot... Step two: Relentlessly dismiss, prune and eliminate. Choose. Ship." ([The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/))

## Structure and formatting

Posts run remarkably short by blog standards: most anchor posts here are two to six paragraphs, several finish under 250 words, and even his longer pieces rarely run past four or five hundred words, with paragraphing compressed further by single-sentence paragraphs used as beats ("So, what to do?" stands alone as its own paragraph in "Don't Shave That Yak!").  Openings almost never set a scene before making a claim: a flat declarative delivered cold ("That would be you." — [The World's Worst Boss](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-worlds-worst-boss/)), a concrete fact already in motion ("Amanda Hocking is making a million dollars a year..." — [Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/)), or a rhetorical question the rest of the piece goes on to answer ("How can I explain the never-ending irrationality of human behavior?" — [Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/)).  There is essentially no throat-clearing paragraph establishing stakes before the argument starts; the claim and the stakes arrive in the same sentence.  Headers are rare on the blog — most posts carry no header beyond the title, because the whole entry functions as a single argument-sized unit — and in the books, sub-headers tend to be short noun phrases or coined terms rather than questions.  Endings turn rather than summarize: a short imperative ("Go, make something. We need it!" — [Are You Making Something?](https://seths.blog/2011/03/are-you-making-something/)), a compact aphorism that reframes the whole post ("Simple doesn't always mean easy." — [The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/)), or a direct question thrown at the reader with no softening ("What are you going to do about it?" — [Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/)).  The books extend the same unit rather than replacing it: [*Linchpin*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7155145-linchpin) and [*This Is Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/40549476-this-is-marketing) are built from many short, self-contained sub-sections — often a page or less — that read like blog posts stitched into chapters, not sustained multi-page essays.

## Reference palette

Everyday commerce and domestic life supply his default examples — waxing a car, a broken garden hose, an EZPass toll, a neighbor's borrowed pillow ([Don't Shave That Yak!](https://seths.blog/2005/03/dont_shave_that/)) — chosen because the reader has lived them, not because they're impressive.  Contemporary, named individuals stand in for aggregate data: Amanda Hocking self-publishing to the Kindle, Rebecca Black reaching an audience with no record label ([Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/)) — proof by specific example, never a cited statistic.  Folk neuroscience is repurposed as everyday metaphor rather than clinical vocabulary: "amygdala" and "lizard brain" describe office procrastination and shipping anxiety, not anything a neuroscientist would recognize as rigorous ([Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/); [*Linchpin*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7155145-linchpin)).  Art and craft supply a second recurring vocabulary — "your art," painters, poets, the working artist who "overcame the resistance" ([*Linchpin*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/7155145-linchpin), via the companion post [Art Is What We Call…](https://seths.blog/2011/02/art-is-what-we-call/)) — borrowed in part from Steven Pressfield's *The War of Art*, whose "resistance" Godin credits by name in "Quieting the Lizard Brain."  His own past books and posts are a fourth source: he quotes and rebuilds his own coined terms decades apart ([*Permission Marketing*](https://seths.blog/2008/01/permission-mark/)'s 1999 definition recurs almost unchanged in [*This Is Marketing*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/40549476-this-is-marketing), 2018).  Notably absent: sports metaphors, war or military imagery (beyond the borrowed, credited "resistance"), and film or celebrity pop-culture references — the palette stays inside commerce, craft, and the reader's own daily errands.

## Sentence-level habits

Sentences run short, and many stand alone as one-clause paragraphs — fragments are used deliberately, not as an error: "No publisher." ([Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/)) A colon frequently introduces a definition or a two-part instruction rather than a list: "Step one: Open all doors." ([The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/)) The flat "X is Y" declarative is his default sentence for defining a term, repeated across the whole corpus regardless of subject: "The Dip is the long slog between starting and mastery" ([*The Dip*](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/excerpt-seth-godins-the-dip-on-the-benefit-of-strategic-quitting/)), "Art is what we call…the thing an artist does" ([Art Is What We Call…](https://seths.blog/2011/02/art-is-what-we-call/)), "A tribe is a group of people connected to one another, connected to a leader, and connected to an idea" ([*Tribes*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/3828382-tribes)). Rhetorical questions are posed and then answered by the whole piece that follows, not resolved in the next clause — "How can I explain the never-ending irrationality of human behavior?" opens "Quieting the Lizard Brain" and isn't answered until the post's final lines. Second-person address is constant in the advice posts, often opening the piece on "you" or "your": "That would be you." ([The World's Worst Boss](https://seths.blog/2010/12/the-worlds-worst-boss/)) Nearly every post contains at least one freestanding, quotable maxim built to be lifted out of context and still make sense on its own: "Strategic quitting is the secret of successful organizations." ([*The Dip*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/19096852-the-dip)) "Simple doesn't always mean easy." ([The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/)) Imperative fragments stack without conjunctions to compress a whole action sequence: "Choose. Ship." ([The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/))

## Signature moves

1. **The Coined Definition.** States "X is Y" as a flat, freestanding definition of a new term, then treats that term as settled vocabulary for the rest of his career. Example: "The Dip is the long slog between starting and mastery." ([*The Dip*, excerpt](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/excerpt-seth-godins-the-dip-on-the-benefit-of-strategic-quitting/))
2. **The Escalating Chain-Reaction Story.** A trivial want triggers an absurd chain of increasingly tangential obligations until the original goal is buried under the detour. Example: the full chain from wanting to wax a car to standing at the zoo shaving a yak. ([Don't Shave That Yak!](https://seths.blog/2005/03/dont_shave_that/))
3. **The Anaphoric List.** A short sentence template repeats with one word changed each time, building a chant-like inventory of a concept's properties instead of a bulleted list. Example: "Great stories make a promise." / "Great stories are trusted." / "Great stories are subtle." ([Ode: How to Tell a Great Story](https://seths.blog/2006/04/ode_how_to_tell/))
4. **The Named-Individual Proof.** A single, named, real person stands in as the entire evidence base for a structural claim about the economy or a platform, in place of aggregate data. Example: "Amanda Hocking is making a million dollars a year publishing her own work to the Kindle. No publisher." ([Reject the Tyranny of Being Picked](https://seths.blog/2011/03/reject-the-tyranny-of-being-picked-pick-yourself/))
5. **The Two-Step Compression.** A whole philosophy of action collapses into a numbered, near-telegraphic two-part instruction, each part a run of imperative fragments. Example: "Step one: Open all doors... Step two: Relentlessly dismiss, prune and eliminate. Choose. Ship." ([The Simple Two-Step Process](https://seths.blog/2011/02/the-simple-two-step-process/))
6. **The Question Thrown Back.** The final line hands the decision back to the reader as an unanswered question, refusing to resolve the piece on the writer's behalf. Example: "Now you've seen the icon and you know its name. What are you going to do about it?" ([Quieting the Lizard Brain](https://seths.blog/2010/01/quieting-the-lizard-brain/))

## What NOT to do (what Godin never does)

- Never cites academic studies, statistics with a named source, or footnotes — claims stand on assertion alone, never "research shows" or "a 2019 study found."
- Never hedges with "I think," "in my opinion," or "maybe" — claims are delivered as settled fact, not floated as personal taste.
- Never opens with scene-setting throat-clearing or an "in today's world" frame — the claim and its stakes arrive in the same sentence the piece opens on.
- Never uses profanity in this corpus — intensity comes from compression and repetition, not from shock language.
- Never reaches for sports, war, or celebrity/pop-culture references as a metaphor source (beyond the openly credited, borrowed "resistance" from Pressfield) — the palette stays inside commerce, craft, and the reader's own errands.
- Never runs long — no anchor post here exceeds a few hundred words, and even book chapters are built from short, self-contained sub-sections rather than sustained multi-page argument.
- Never leaves a piece on mere description — every post ends on an aphorism, a command, or a question aimed straight at the reader.
- Never re-explains a coined term once it has been introduced — "lizard brain," "the Dip," "permission," and "the smallest viable market" are used as settled vocabulary from their first appearance onward, with no refresher for new readers.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Against Steven Pressfield's *The War of Art* — the direct source of "the resistance," which Godin credits by name — Pressfield sustains a book-length, near-mystical military allegory around a single antagonist, while Godin borrows the same vocabulary and redeploys it in three-hundred-word bursts aimed at a business reader, trading Pressfield's sustained mysticism for aphoristic compression.  Against Malcolm Gladwell, who builds long-form narrative nonfiction from journalistic reporting, named studies, and multi-scene set pieces, Godin strips out the reporting almost entirely and substitutes a flat assertion the reader is expected to test against their own experience, not a citation trail.  Against Simon Sinek, whose "Start With Why" register builds one big idea across a sustained argument with narrative scaffolding (Apple, the Wright Brothers), Godin distrusts that scaffolding and prefers dozens of independent, self-contained hits to one extended argument.  Against Ryan Holiday, who grounds Stoic advice in extended historical anecdote — a general, an emperor — Godin's proof is almost always a contemporary, ordinary, named business actor (a self-published Kindle author, a trivia-game vendor), never a figure borrowed from history.
