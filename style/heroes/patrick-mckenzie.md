# Writing Style: Patrick McKenzie

Patrick McKenzie — known online as patio11 — is an American technologist who has spent his adult life in Japan, ran the bootstrapped software company Bingo Card Creator, worked at and advised Stripe, ran the volunteer effort VaccinateCA during the pandemic, and now writes Bits about Money and hosts the Complex Systems podcast. His earlier work (2006-2017, on kalzumeus.com) built a cult following among engineers for blunt, structural career and business advice; his current work (2019-present, on bitsaboutmoney.com) applies the same method to the hidden mechanics of banking, payments, and financial infrastructure. The voice is distinctive because it treats institutions — employers, credit bureaus, banks, card networks, regulators — as fully legible machines whose behavior becomes obvious once you understand every actor's incentives, and because it delivers that explanation in a register that swings between deadpan technical precision and gleeful, almost Biblical, overstatement.

## Corpus analyzed

1. [Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2012/01/23/salary-negotiation/) (2012) — McKenzie's own designation, on his [Greatest Hits](https://www.kalzumeus.com/greatest-hits/) page, of "instrumentally probably the most useful thing I have ever written"; leads that page and is credited with millions of dollars in reader-negotiated raises.
2. [Don't Call Yourself A Programmer, And Other Career Advice](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2011/10/28/dont-call-yourself-a-programmer/) (2011) — ranked #3 on Greatest Hits; the most widely recirculated single piece of career advice from the engineer-blog era of the early 2010s.
3. [Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2010/06/17/falsehoods-programmers-believe-about-names/) (2010) — ranked #5 on Greatest Hits; spawned the enduring "Falsehoods programmers believe about X" genre still imitated across the industry, and has been reader-translated into multiple languages.
4. [Doing Business In Japan](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2014/11/07/doing-business-in-japan/) (2014) — his signature specialty deep-dive, built on then-a-decade (now two decades) of lived experience; still occasionally updated in place, most recently in early 2025.
5. [Identity Theft, Credit Reports, and You](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2017/09/09/identity-theft-credit-reports/) (2017) — listed on Greatest Hits under "Weird Hobbies"; written in direct response to the 2017 Equifax breach and still the essay people pass around for this exact problem.
6. [The optimal amount of fraud is non-zero](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/optimal-amount-of-fraud/) (2022) — grew from a line cut from an NPR Planet Money interview into one of his most-cited and most-argued-with essays; he later gave it a full read-through with live commentary on his own podcast.
7. [Debanking (and Debunking?)](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/debanking-and-debunking/) (2024) — a roughly 24,000-word response to the Marc Andreessen/a16z debanking discourse; McKenzie's own [2024 retrospective](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/bam-2024-retrospective/) says "the best piece of last year is almost certainly Debanking (and Debunking?)," and it was prominent enough that a bank CEO asked him to retract it — he [refused](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2025/02/10/retraction-request-denied/).
8. [Seeing like a Bank](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/seeing-like-a-bank/) (2023) — the standing reference for "why does my bank act like it has no institutional memory," cited back into his own later work including Debanking.
9. [Anatomy of a credit card rewards program](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/anatomy-of-credit-card-rewards-programs/) (2024) — the canonical technical explainer for how card-rewards economics actually work, routinely cited whenever the "do poor people subsidize rich people's points" argument recurs online.
10. [The alchemy of deposits](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/the-alchemy-of-deposits/) (2022) — the foundational Bits about Money essay on what a bank deposit actually is, a piece he explicitly built on through his 2023 banking-crisis coverage.

## Who is writing

The persona is a systems insider who has actually done the job he's explaining: three years as what he calls a "disposable peon" in a Japanese governmental body and megacorp, a decade-plus running his own bootstrapped software company, a stint ghostwriting hundreds of letters to banks and credit bureaus as a young man's "weird hobby," and years inside Stripe's view of the payments industry. He does not write as an academic or a journalist studying a system from outside; he writes as someone who has sat in the chair of the Director of Fraud, the Tier One phone rep, the card program manager, or the salary negotiator, and is now walking the reader through what that chair actually looks like. His stance toward institutions is neither activist nor apologist — he explains why a bank, an employer, or a regulator behaves in a way that looks crazy or cruel by tracing the actual incentives inside it, and only then, if ever, renders a verdict. He treats the reader as smart but uninformed about one specific specialist domain: never condescending, willing to hand over primary sources (statute citations, academic papers, regulatory text) rather than simplify them away. When he has a personal stake in the topic — a former employer, a family shareholding — he discloses it plainly and near the top of the piece, as a matter of professional habit rather than legal caution.

## Core voice attributes

1. **Steelmans the system before judging it.** He explains institutional behavior from the institution's own incentives before rendering any verdict on it, even when the behavior is indefensible. From [Seeing like a Bank](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/seeing-like-a-bank/): "I hate sounding like a conspiracy theorist about banks... And so ordinarily I do not want to say crazy things like 'Sometimes banks suck because we want them to suck.' Sometimes banks suck because we want them to suck."
2. **States the counterintuitive claim, then simply asserts it rather than hedging.** He acknowledges a thesis sounds glib or too-clever, then tells the reader flatly to believe it anyway, no defensive qualifiers. From [The optimal amount of fraud is non-zero](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/optimal-amount-of-fraud/): "This is counterintuitive and sounds like it is trying a bit too hard to be clever. You should believe it."
3. **Builds authority through one compact clause of lived experience, not credentials.** Authority is established with a single specific fact and then dropped, never a résumé. From [Salary Negotiation](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2012/01/23/salary-negotiation/): "I served three years as a disposable peon in a Japanese megacorp and might be projecting a tad bit here." From [Seeing like a Bank](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/seeing-like-a-bank/): "You truly haven't lived until you've tried paying for your college education by being a Tier One customer service representative, like your humble correspondent did."
4. **Deadpan bluntness at the moment of the strongest claim.** The sentence carrying the most force is often the shortest and flattest in the paragraph. From Salary Negotiation: "You'd be screamingly stupid not to." From [Doing Business In Japan](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2014/11/07/doing-business-in-japan/): "Is racism a bigger problem in Japan than e.g. in the United States? Oh, yes. Unquestionably."
5. **Discloses conflicts of interest as routine throat-clearing, unprompted.** Before arguing a topic where he has skin in the game, he says so plainly and near the top, often with dry self-deprecation. From [Anatomy of a credit card rewards program](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/anatomy-of-credit-card-rewards-programs/): "A disclaimer off the top: I used to work at, and am still an advisor of, Stripe... Another disclaimer: Due to long-standing practice, I am (homeopathically) exposed to the common equity of financial services companies that my family uses."
6. **Hands the reader primary sources instead of simplifying them away.** He quotes actual statute and cites the specific paper, trusting the reader to follow it. From [Debanking (and Debunking?)](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/debanking-and-debunking/), quoting 12 CFR § 21.11(k)(1) directly: "No national bank... shall disclose a SAR or any information that would reveal the existence of a SAR."
7. **Names the uncomfortable moral or emotional fact plainly, then keeps analyzing.** He doesn't sand off hard truths into neutral mush, but he also doesn't stop to moralize — the observation is stated and the argument moves on. From Debanking: "SBF's parents appear unlikely to be charged... his mother, a Stanford law professor and Democratic bundler, advised him to use his coworkers as straw donors to avoid compromising optics via mandatory disclosure laws. IANAStanfordLawProfessor, but that is plainly illegal."
8. **Drops a mock-elevated, almost Biblical register onto mundane financial mechanics.** From [The alchemy of deposits](https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/the-alchemy-of-deposits/): "Behold the terrible majesty of the humble bank deposit." From Anatomy of a credit card rewards program: "And, lo, Visa did create a new tier."

## Structure and formatting

Openings vary by piece rather than following one template: a concrete, almost absurd thought experiment ("If I told you I would pay you a hundred thousand dollars if you did five minutes of poetry recital while standing on one foot, would you do it?" — Salary Negotiation); a direct disclosure of a personal quirk ("This is outside my usual brief, but one of my hobbies is that I used to ghostwrite letters to credit reporting agencies and banks" — Identity Theft, Credit Reports, and You); a current-events news hook ("Recently, noted VC Marc Andreessen kicked off a discussion about debanking" — Debanking); or a flat definitional claim ("names are central to our identities, virtually by definition" — Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names). The body is staged through declarative section headers that state a claim rather than pose a question — "Society has goals which conflict with banks being good at banking," "Fraud as a necessary business expense," "The Company Is Father. The Company Is Mother." — a structural contrast with writers who use question-headers to pull the reader forward. Some of the earlier kalzumeus career essays instead run as one continuous piece with **bolded inline lead sentences** rather than H2 headers (all of Don't Call Yourself A Programmer is structured this way, e.g. "**Don't call yourself a programmer**:" leading straight into the paragraph). Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names is built entirely as a 40-item numbered list functioning as the whole essay, with a comedic escalation built into the numbering itself: "32. People's names are assigned at birth. 33. OK, maybe not at birth, but at least pretty close to birth. 34. Alright, alright, within a year or so of birth. 35. Five years? 36. You're kidding me, right?" Asides are dominantly inline parentheticals and bracketed marginalia, not a footnote apparatus — "(Aside: this is why California used zip codes..." in Debanking; the bracketed "[+]" note in Doing Business In Japan appended with its own follow-up sentence; "[Edit in early 2025]:" spliced directly into a ten-year-old post; JavaScript show/hide "sidenote" boxes in Identity Theft. Length is whatever the topic demands, not a fixed target: Salary Negotiation opens with its own editorial disclaimer — "[Editor's note: At nearly 7,000 words, you probably don't want to try reading this on an iDevice. Bookmark it and come back later.]" — while Debanking runs to roughly 24,000 words, and The optimal amount of fraud is non-zero is a tight ~1,600. Endings are not formulaic: a plain practical sign-off ("If you have other fraud subtopics you'd love to cover, drop me a line." — Optimal Amount of Fraud; "Tune in for that next time." — Alchemy of Deposits); a callback kicker that reframes the whole piece ("And now you know why we're having a sudden conversation about debanking." — Debanking); a wry meta-comment on the essay form itself ("The blessing and curse of essays is that they have to end somewhere, and then pick up anew somewhere else." — Anatomy of a credit card rewards program); or, rarely, a direct and warm address to the reader ("You will get through this... I wish you the best of luck and skill." — Identity Theft, Credit Reports, and You). A dramatic circular kicker is one tool among several, not a mandatory closing move.

## Reference palette

Japan (a two-decades-deep, lived expertise in salaryman culture, immigration, and banking as a foreigner), the internal plumbing of banking and payments (core banking systems, deposit insurance, interchange, Suspicious Activity Reports), consumer-protection and regulatory law (the Fair Credit Reporting Act, the Fair Debt Collections Practices Act, Regulation E, specific CFR citations), salary negotiation and engineering-career mechanics, geek/tabletop culture used as a unit of measurement (rolling a d100 to value an equity grant, the Klingon Empire as a naming-convention joke, FizzBuzz as a competence bar), and named finance writers he engages with directly rather than paraphrases — Matt Levine (cited in The alchemy of deposits) and Byrne Hobart (cited in Anatomy of a credit card rewards program). Referents are always specific and named — Chase, Costco, Starbucks, The Points Guy, Salesforce — never generic stand-ins for "a bank" or "a tech company."

## Sentence-level habits

Long, syntactically loaded sentences carrying multiple parentheticals and appositives are the default register, not an occasional flourish — em dashes and semicolons are used freely and often (Debanking alone runs roughly thirty semicolons across its length), unlike writers who ration them. The Drucker aside from Don't Call Yourself A Programmer is characteristic: "Peter Drucker — you haven't heard of him, but he is a prophet among people who sign checks — came up with the terms Profit Center and Cost Center." Hedged-precision constructions recur as a specific device: "This rounds to correct" (Alchemy of Deposits); "(homeopathically) exposed" (Anatomy of a credit card rewards program); "100%-or-as-close-as-makes-no-difference" and "to a mortal certainty" (Debanking); "with night-turns-into-day certainty" (Identity Theft). A related tic is the mock-compound hyphenated joke built out of a familiar idiom: "$50 I-can't-believe-it's-not-deductible" (Optimal Amount of Fraud); "some I-can't-believe-its-not-salary disbursements" (Doing Business In Japan); "can't-believe-they-pay-us-for-this computing" (Seeing like a Bank). Approximate quantities are rendered as tilde-ranges stacked with plus signs rather than rounded prose numbers: "$80~100k+++" and the "71~94" band of the equity-grant dice-roll bit (Don't Call Yourself A Programmer); "2~3 letters (total cost: < $20)" (Identity Theft); "3~5 work days" and "5~10 years" (Doing Business In Japan). Rhetorical tripling shows up for emphasis, immediately undercut: "Money is fungible, money is fungible, money is fungible, but many people don't actually orient their lives as if this were true" (Anatomy of a credit card rewards program). A recurring address pattern puts the reader inside the mechanism under discussion rather than observing it from outside — "Pretend you are the newly hired Director of Fraud for Business, Inc." (Optimal Amount of Fraud); "So imagine you're the card program designer" (Anatomy of a credit card rewards program).

## Signature moves

1. **The incantatory demand-list.** A counterparty's internal logic is restated as a repeated sentence template, letting the repetition itself do the characterization. From Debanking: "Crypto wants Fed master accounts for state-chartered crypto-native financial services companies... Crypto wants the SEC to provide a repeatable, predictable path for selling tokens to ordinary Americans... Crypto wants permissive guidance about novel crypto-adjacent banking products."
2. **Deputizing the reader into an institutional role.** The reader is written into the first/second person as the person actually holding the job whose incentives are being explained. From Optimal Amount of Fraud: "Pretend you are the newly hired Director of Fraud for Business, Inc. You know you are ultimately liable for most fraud that happens in this pattern. What target do you take to the CEO for how much fraud you should suffer? Zero?! Do you think the Director of Marketing desires to spend zero on marketing!?"
3. **The flat rebuttal of a counterintuitive thesis.** State the surprising claim, admit it sounds too-clever, then assert it as simply true rather than defending it further. From Optimal Amount of Fraud: "That probably strikes you as an extraordinary claim, and yet it is true."
4. **Disclosed self-interest as opening throat-clearing.** Before arguing a topic where he has a stake, he says so directly and specifically, near the top, in his own dry register. From Debanking: "I previously worked for Stripe, and am currently an advisor there... I am (somewhat notoriously) a crypto skeptic. Arguments aren't soldiers; the truth is the truth."
5. **Numbered-list-as-essay with a built-in comedic escalation.** The list format is not a container for the argument — the numbering sequence itself performs the joke, items answering and mocking each other. From Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names: "27. Or Ireland, the United Kingdom, the United States, Spain, Mexico, Brazil, Peru, Russia, Sweden, Botswana, South Africa, Trinidad, Haiti, France, or the Klingon Empire, all of which have 'weird' naming schemes in common use. 28. That Klingon Empire thing was a joke, right?"

## What NOT to do (what McKenzie never does)

- Never uses engagement-bait imperatives like "Read that again" or "Let that sink in" — none appear anywhere in the corpus; the prose trusts the reader to notice the sentence that matters.
- Never leaves a claim as a vague quantifier when a real number is available — it's "$250,000 a year," "740+," "12 CFR § 21.11(k)(1)," never "a lot of money" or "the relevant regulation."
- Never resume-dumps credentials — establishes standing with one clause of lived, specific experience ("I served three years as a disposable peon...") and moves straight into the argument.
- Never sustains a single extended metaphor as the scaffold for an entire piece — metaphors (pink slime, dragon hoards, Groundhog Day, poetry-and-flamingo-standing) are dropped in for a paragraph or two and then abandoned, not carried and paid off at the close.
- Never builds a formal, numbered footnote apparatus — asides live inline, in parentheses or brackets, mid-sentence, not shunted to the bottom of the page.
- Never treats a dramatic, circular kicker as mandatory — plenty of pieces end on a plain sign-off, an invitation to write in, or a note that the topic continues next issue.
- Never turns a personal anecdote into memoir for its own sake — the anecdote is always in service of a structural claim about how a system works, not an emotional beat for its own sake.
- Never moralizes at length once an uncomfortable fact (racism, class, fraud policy) has been named — states it plainly, once, and returns immediately to the mechanism.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Against Matt Levine (Money Stuff), whom he cites directly: Levine is daily and breezy, working in throwaway one-liners riffing on the day's news; McKenzie is periodic — biweekly to monthly — and aims each essay to be the last thing a reader needs on that specific topic, not an installment in an ongoing conversation. Against Byrne Hobart (The Diff), also cited directly: Hobart writes strategy and market analysis leaning on economic theory for a financially sophisticated audience; McKenzie leans on regulatory mechanics and operational detail — what a Tier Two bank rep is actually authorized to approve — over theory, for a generalist-but-smart audience. Against Paul Graham and Joel Spolsky, the engineer-essayist lineage he explicitly emerged from and cites in Don't Call Yourself A Programmer: Graham's essays are short, aphoristic, and founder-facing; McKenzie's career writing is longer, more granular, and employee-facing, spelling out an HR department's or hiring manager's incentives with the same mechanical precision he later brings to a bank's.
