# Writing Style: Joel Spolsky

Joel Spolsky is a Microsoft alumnus turned software entrepreneur — a Program Manager on the Excel team from 1991 to 1994, founder of Fog Creek Software in 2000, co-founder of Stack Overflow with Jeff Atwood in 2008, and creator of Trello in 2011 — who wrote joelonsoftware.com from 2000 into the mid-2000s as a working practitioner's running commentary on how software actually gets designed, built, sold, and staffed.  The voice is distinctive because it refuses both the academic paper and the conference keynote: he teaches through worked technical examples, dated personal anecdotes with named colleagues, and blunt, capitalized "laws" that escaped the blog and became industry shorthand — leaky abstractions, architecture astronauts, the Joel Test.  It reads like a teacher's patience wedded to a founder's impatience with anything that doesn't ship.

## Corpus analyzed

1. [The Law of Leaky Abstractions](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/) (2002) — sits on joelonsoftware.com's own curated "Top 10" category and on independent "best of" retrospectives; coined a term that is now standard CS vocabulary.
2. [Things You Should Never Do, Part I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/) (2000) — the canonical "never rewrite from scratch" argument, still invoked in Hacker News threads about software rewrites two decades later.
3. [The Joel Test: 12 Steps to Better Code](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/08/09/the-joel-test-12-steps-to-better-code/) (2000) — his single most operationally useful piece; still spawns "what would a 2024 Joel Test look like" discussion threads.
4. [The Absolute Minimum Every Software Developer Absolutely, Positively Must Know About Unicode and Character Sets (No Excuses!)](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2003/10/08/the-absolute-minimum-every-software-developer-absolutely-positively-must-know-about-unicode-and-character-sets-no-excuses/) (2003) — on both curated lists; remains the internet's default answer to "what is Unicode, actually."
5. [Don't Let Architecture Astronauts Scare You](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2001/04/21/dont-let-architecture-astronauts-scare-you/) (2001) — coined "architecture astronaut," a term with its own Wikipedia entry and recurring 20-years-later retrospectives.
6. [How Microsoft Lost the API War](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2004/06/13/how-microsoft-lost-the-api-war/) (2004) — on both curated lists; widely cited as his best long-form business-strategy essay.
7. [The Iceberg Secret, Revealed](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/02/13/the-iceberg-secret-revealed/) (2002) — a repeat pick in independent "top 10" retrospectives for its account of how non-programmers judge software.
8. [Strategy Letter I: Ben and Jerry's vs. Amazon](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/05/12/strategy-letter-i-ben-and-jerrys-vs-amazon/) (2000) — the opening entry in his recurring "Strategy Letter" series, a standard reading-list pick for business-model thinking.
9. [The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/03/23/the-guerrilla-guide-to-interviewing/) (2000, revised through Version 3.0) — rewritten twice and expanded into the book *Smart and Gets Things Done*; the standard citation for technical-hiring philosophy.
10. [My First BillG Review](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/06/16/my-first-billg-review/) (2006) — his most-cited pure narrative essay, read as much for craft as for content.

## Who is writing

The persona is a former Microsoft Program Manager (Excel Basic, 1991–94) who left to found his own small software company and never stopped writing like someone still accountable for a ship date.  He grew up partly in Albuquerque and partly in Israel, where he served in the Paratroopers Brigade and helped found a kibbutz, before returning to the U.S. to finish a CS degree at Yale — but the corpus almost never leans on that biography for authority; credibility comes instead from having sat in the room (a BillG review, a Netscape-style rewrite postmortem, a hiring loop), not from a résumé line.  He writes as a peer talking to other builders, not a guru talking down to an audience: the implied reader is a working programmer, PM, or founder who is busy and half-skeptical, so the essay has to earn its length by teaching something usable before it's allowed to be clever.  He treats the market — wages, market share, whether a product ships — as the ultimate judge of any argument, which places him closer to an engineer-turned-operator than to a philosopher; when he's wrong or conflicted he says so plainly rather than hedging into mush, and when he's right he says that plainly too.

## Core voice attributes

1. **States the verdict before the argument.** Spolsky opens with the conclusion as a flat declarative, then spends the essay proving it — he doesn't build up to a reveal, he leads with it. In "The Law of Leaky Abstractions" he asserts within the first few paragraphs: "All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky." ([The Law of Leaky Abstractions](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/)) Likewise "Things You Should Never Do, Part I" names the sin outright before explaining it: "They decided to rewrite the code from scratch." ([Things You Should Never Do, Part I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/))

2. **Builds the case from a worked technical example, not from citation.** Rather than reference research or other writers, he walks the reader through an actual mechanism until the point becomes self-evident. The abstraction essay spends its opening movement on how TCP behaves on top of unreliable IP: "TCP is built on top of IP. In other words, TCP is obliged to somehow send data reliably using only an unreliable tool." ([The Law of Leaky Abstractions](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/)) The Unicode essay does the same with byte encodings before it ever mentions Unicode by name.

3. **Tests every claim against market economics.** Arguments resolve to wages, market share, or profit, not taste. On the death of Windows API programming: "Windows API programmers...earn about $130,000 a year, while typical Web programmers...earn about $80,000 a year." ([How Microsoft Lost the API War](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2004/06/13/how-microsoft-lost-the-api-war/)) On rewrites: "You are throwing away your market leadership." ([Things You Should Never Do, Part I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/))

4. **Names the phenomenon and capitalizes it into reusable shorthand.** He doesn't just describe a pattern — he coins a Proper Noun for it that the rest of the essay (and later essays) can point back to: "These are the people I call Architecture Astronauts." ([Don't Let Architecture Astronauts Scare You](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2001/04/21/dont-let-architecture-astronauts-scare-you/)) Same move with "This is what I call a leaky abstraction" ([The Law of Leaky Abstractions](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/)) and "The secret is that People Who Aren't Programmers Do Not Understand This." ([The Iceberg Secret, Revealed](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/02/13/the-iceberg-secret-revealed/))

5. **Uses cartoonish, absurdist threats as rhetorical punctuation.** When a point needs emphasis, he inflates the consequence to something surreal rather than raising his voice. On ignoring character encodings: "I'm going to punish you by making you peel onions for 6 months in a submarine." ([The Absolute Minimum Every Software Developer... Unicode](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2003/10/08/the-absolute-minimum-every-software-developer-absolutely-positively-must-know-about-unicode-and-character-sets-no-excuses/))

6. **Grounds lessons in dated, named-colleague personal anecdote, not composite scenes.** The BillG review essay places a real date, a real coworker, and real dialogue in service of a general lesson about technical competence in leadership: "'Good guess, but no cigar,' Ed said, and left me wondering for a while." ([My First BillG Review](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/06/16/my-first-billg-review/))

7. **Forces a binary decision; refuses to let "maybe" stand.** Nuance gets collapsed into a two-way fork the reader must actually pick. On hiring: "If you can't tell, that means No Hire." ([The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/03/23/the-guerrilla-guide-to-interviewing/)) On business models: "The worst thing you can do is fail to decide." ([Strategy Letter I: Ben and Jerry's vs. Amazon](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/05/12/strategy-letter-i-ben-and-jerrys-vs-amazon/))

8. **Undercuts his own authority with a plain, unadorned aside.** Not a hedge, but a brief admission that his own preference or prior claim doesn't settle the matter: "I'm actually a little bit sad about this, myself." ([How Microsoft Lost the API War](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2004/06/13/how-microsoft-lost-the-api-war/)) and "I know, it's not just Raymond, of course" ([How Microsoft Lost the API War](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2004/06/13/how-microsoft-lost-the-api-war/))

9. **Commands the reader directly, in the imperative.** He tells the reader what to do rather than suggesting it: "Never lower your standards no matter how hard it seems to find great candidates." ([The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/03/23/the-guerrilla-guide-to-interviewing/)) and, after a list of common mistakes, simply: "Please, just stop doing this." ([The Joel Test: 12 Steps to Better Code](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/08/09/the-joel-test-12-steps-to-better-code/))

## Structure and formatting

Pieces open one of two ways: a flat declarative claim delivered cold ("Building a company? You've got one very important decision to make" — [Strategy Letter I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/05/12/strategy-letter-i-ben-and-jerrys-vs-amazon/)), or a concrete scene already in motion (a CEO frustrated with his team, a Program Manager starting a job on a specific date) — he essentially never opens with a rhetorical question or a scholarly framing of the problem.  Arguments are staged as a sequence of worked examples that escalate in stakes, not as claim-then-evidence paragraphs: the Unicode piece moves ASCII to code pages to DBCS to Unicode theory to UTF-8, each stage breaking the previous one's assumption.  Headers are sparse and functional rather than rhetorical — no question-headers, no wordplay headers; the numbered list is reserved for practical, checklist-shaped content (the Joel Test's twelve items, the Guerrilla Guide's interview stages), while argumentative essays (Leaky Abstractions, Architecture Astronauts) run as continuous prose with almost no subheadings at all.  Emphasis is used sparingly and functionally: italics mark a term's first, technical use ("_used_," "_tested_," "_significantly_"), and ALL CAPS is reserved for the rare moment of genuine shouted emphasis ("IT'S NOT THAT HARD," "HE HAD READ THE WHOLE GODDAMNED THING AND WRITTEN NOTES IN THE MARGINS") rather than for routine bolding of claims.  Endings are frequently anticlimactic on purpose — not a triumphant kicker but a quiet, deflated admission, sometimes a single word: "My First BillG Review" closes its whole reflective coda on one syllable, "Still." ([My First BillG Review](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/06/16/my-first-billg-review/)) — and "How Microsoft Lost the API War" ends on melancholy acceptance rather than a victory lap.

## Reference palette

Microsoft's internal culture and org chart, named and specific — Raymond Chen, MSDN Magazine, BillG reviews, the SimCity compatibility hack, the Excel Basic spec — used as primary evidence, not color.  Programming and computer-science internals and history: TCP/IP, memory pointers, ASCII and code pages, C++ strings, NFS.  Business economics and strategy: wage comparisons, market share, capital requirements, Ben & Jerry's versus Amazon as an archetype pair.  Absurdist, cartoonish gags used to puncture pomposity: onions in a submarine, a hijacked cruise ship demanding "a GPL implementation of WATFIV" ([The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/10/25/the-guerrilla-guide-to-interviewing-version-30-now-shipping/)), astronauts running out of oxygen.  Ironic religious and messianic imagery deployed to mock hype: new architectures are "not, I repeat, not, a good substitute for the messiah riding his white ass into Jerusalem" ([Don't Let Architecture Astronauts Scare You](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2001/04/21/dont-let-architecture-astronauts-scare-you/)).  Physical, bodily analogies for incompetence: "watching non-programmers trying to run software companies is like watching someone who doesn't know how to surf trying to surf" ([My First BillG Review](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/06/16/my-first-billg-review/)).  Notably absent from this corpus: film, literature, or pop-music references — the palette stays inside the workplace, the market, and the machine.

## Sentence-level habits

Rhythm alternates a long, clause-heavy technical sentence with a short, blunt verdict sentence immediately after it — the short sentence lands as punctuation for the long one, not decoration: "Gotcha!" and "Oops." both function this way as single-word paragraph breaks in [Strategy Letter I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/05/12/strategy-letter-i-ben-and-jerrys-vs-amazon/).  Direct second-person address is constant in the advice pieces, opening whole essays on "you": "Building a company? You've got one very important decision to make" ([Strategy Letter I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/05/12/strategy-letter-i-ben-and-jerrys-vs-amazon/)).  Rhetorical questions are almost always self-answered within the same breath, not left hanging — "In design questions, what are you looking for?" is answered in the next sentence, not left as a mic-drop ([The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](https://www.cse.unr.edu/~sushil/researchAdvice/GIV1.html)).  Parallel repetition of a short sentence frame builds momentum: "Old code has been _used_. It has been _tested_." ([Things You Should Never Do, Part I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/)).  Invented terms are capitalized like proper nouns throughout an essay once coined — Architecture Astronauts, the Iceberg Secret, Get Big Fast — so the reader can track them as recurring characters in the argument rather than one-off phrases.  Concrete numbers are deployed as rhetorical exclamation points rather than mere data — a 500-page spec, "$130,000 a year" versus "$80,000 a year," "3 minutes" for the Joel Test — never a vague "a lot of money" or "quite a while."  Profanity appears almost exclusively as reported speech about someone else, not in his own narrating voice — the joke in "My First BillG Review" is that a colleague's whole job during the meeting was tallying how many times Bill Gates said the F-word, not that Spolsky himself curses.

## Signature moves

1. **The Coined Law.** He turns an observed pattern into a named, capitalized rule that survives the essay and gets cited by name afterward. Example: "This is what I call a leaky abstraction." ([The Law of Leaky Abstractions](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/))
2. **The Binary Verdict.** Nuance is deliberately collapsed into a forced two-way choice with no room for "it depends." Example: "If you can't tell, that means No Hire." ([The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/03/23/the-guerrilla-guide-to-interviewing/))
3. **The Insider Anecdote as Proof.** A specific, dated, named-colleague scene from his own career stands in for a general claim, in place of a study or survey. Example: "HE HAD READ THE WHOLE GODDAMNED THING AND WRITTEN NOTES IN THE MARGINS." ([My First BillG Review](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/06/16/my-first-billg-review/))
4. **The Absurdist Threat.** A mundane technical failure gets inflated into a cartoonish, physically impossible punishment rather than a stern warning. Example: "I'm going to punish you by making you peel onions for 6 months in a submarine." ([The Absolute Minimum Every Software Developer... Unicode](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2003/10/08/the-absolute-minimum-every-software-developer-absolutely-positively-must-know-about-unicode-and-character-sets-no-excuses/))
5. **The Deflating Question.** A big rhetorical question gets asked and then immediately dismissed as trivial or obvious, denying it the weight it seemed to promise. Example: "Whoopee. But that's about as interesting to me as learning that my supermarket uses trucks to get things from the warehouse. Yawn." ([Don't Let Architecture Astronauts Scare You](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2001/04/21/dont-let-architecture-astronauts-scare-you/))
6. **The Melancholy Understatement Close.** Instead of a triumphant kicker, the essay ends on a quiet, almost sad admission — sometimes a single word. Example: the entire closing reflection of "My First BillG Review" resolves to "Still." ([My First BillG Review](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2006/06/16/my-first-billg-review/))

## What NOT to do (what Spolsky never does)

- Never hedges a conclusion into mush with endless "on the other hand" qualifiers — he commits to a verdict and states it as a flat law, not an opinion.
- Never builds an argument's backbone from academic citation or third-party studies — the evidence is his own lived experience, named colleagues, and economic logic, not a literature review.
- Never uses buzzwords or corporate jargon un-ironically — when COM+, SOAP, or Hailstorm appear, it's to mock the hype around them, never to signal fluency.
- Never softens a claim with "I think" or "in my opinion" — claims are stated as fact, then defended, not floated as personal taste.
- Never leaves the practical takeaway implicit — a piece always closes with a concrete instruction, number, or decision, never just a mood.
- Never dramatizes or invents scene detail in a personal anecdote — real dates, real names, real dialogue; if he doesn't remember it, he doesn't stage it.
- Never curses in his own narrating voice within this corpus — profanity, when it appears, belongs to someone he's quoting.
- Never reaches outside the workplace, the market, and the machine for its reference palette — no film references, no literary allusions, no borrowed frameworks from psychology or physics.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Compared with Paul Graham, who builds sweeping first-principles generalizations through abstract meditation, Spolsky stays tethered to one dated, specific, named incident at a time and forces it toward a binary practical verdict rather than a philosophical claim.  Compared with Jeff Atwood (Coding Horror), who aggregates and cites external studies, other blogs, and data visualizations to build a case, Spolsky's evidence is almost entirely personal experience and market economics — he rarely quotes another writer at all.  Compared with a polemicist like DHH, who picks fights and escalates them, Spolsky is didactic and patient — the Unicode essay teaches ASCII history before it will let itself discuss Unicode — even when the underlying claim is just as contrarian.
