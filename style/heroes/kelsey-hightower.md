# Writing Style: Kelsey Hightower

Kelsey Hightower is a software engineer, longtime Kubernetes evangelist, and former Google Distinguished Engineer whose reputation was built almost entirely outside the conventional blog post — through terse GitHub READMEs, live on-stage demos, and hours of unscripted podcast conversation, which is why this doc leans heavily on transcripts rather than essays. He has no personal blog and few long-form written pieces; the closest things to a manifesto are two GitHub repositories (`kubernetes-the-hard-way` and `nocode`) whose entire argument is made through terseness and dry deadpan rather than exposition. The distinctiveness of the voice is that it is almost anti-literary: no metaphor scaffolding, no essayistic throat-clearing, no hedging-for-effect — just plain declarative sentences, a demo instead of an argument, and analogies borrowed from ordinary adult life (fast food jobs, car payments, home electrical work, magic tricks) rather than from literature or pop culture. Because so much of the corpus is spoken, this document is unusually careful to mark which artifacts are written prose and which are transcribed speech, since the two registers differ (spoken Hightower rambles, circles, and self-interrupts; written Hightower is almost aggressively minimal).

## Who is writing

The voice belongs to a self-taught engineer for whom technology was, in his own words from the ReadME Podcast, "a means of survival": "my job before tech was like fast food. And so for me, getting into technology was like a means of survival. So self-taught was the preferred option, mainly because it was the most accessible option" (*Kelsey Hightower — Present*, GitHub ReadME Podcast, 2023). He describes learning as being "taught by authors you've never met," and tells a formative story about acing a job interview on FreeBSD fundamentals alone: "I do know Unix, but not Linux specifically... And just so happened I hit the lottery because he had a free BSD tattoo on his leg" (same source). He went on to become the public face of Kubernetes at Google — first as a developer advocate, later a Distinguished Engineer — and retired from the company at 42, a decision he narrates candidly and at length in the Changelog transcripts rather than treating as a private matter. His stance toward the audience is that of a translator standing between a genuinely complex system and a beginner who has been made to feel it's out of reach: "the fundamentals are more important than people give credit for" and "when you understand what you're doing, you can automate if you want to" (Changelog, "Automation is the serialization of understanding," 2022, adapted from *Ship It!* #44). He treats his own biography — poverty, materialism, burnout, representation — as raw teaching material rather than as memoir, always turning the personal story back into a lesson the listener can use.

## Core voice attributes

1. **Radical minimalism as the argument itself.** The clearest demonstration is the `nocode` README, where the entire joke is delivered through empty code fences: "No code is the best way to write secure and reliable applications. Write nothing; deploy nowhere... Start by not writing any code." followed by a literally empty code block, then "Yep. That's it." and later "I know right?" (`nocode` README, github.com/kelseyhightower/nocode). The humor is 100% structural — there is no punchline sentence, only the absence where content should be.
2. **Teach by making the reader do the work, not by explaining it to them.** He is explicit that `kubernetes-the-hard-way` was a deliberate rejection of a shortcut: "I decided that we didn't need more software. We needed some docs... I wanted people to copy and paste every command, tediously, multiple times, because I wanted them to know how it all fit together... when you see a magician do a magic trick and they show you how they did it, it's not magic anymore. It's just a trick" (*Kelsey Hightower — Present*, GitHub ReadME Podcast, 2023). The README itself states the same idea in one flat sentence: "This guide is not for someone looking for a fully automated tool to bring up a Kubernetes cluster. Kubernetes The Hard Way is optimized for learning, which means taking the long route" (`kubernetes-the-hard-way` README).
3. **Success is measured by his own obsolescence.** Rather than defending Kubernetes's importance, he routinely argues for its disappearance. Spoken, on the ReadME Podcast: "the future of Kubernetes is, if we're being honest, that it has to go away. And if it goes away, that's a sign of progress" (*Kelsey Hightower — Present*, 2023). The same idea, reordered into a tighter written pull-quote by the editors of [The GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/2023-05-24-kelsey-hightower-on-leadership-in-open-source-and-the-future-of-kubernetes/): "The future of Kubernetes, if we're being honest, is that it has to go away. If we're still talking about Kubernetes 20 years from now, that would be a sad moment in tech because we didn't come up with any better ideas." The same reflex shows up earlier, framed as a correction of a popular misreading: "It's not the end game," he says. Rather, at some point, it increasingly becomes "the new platform for building other platforms" (Opensource.com, Jan 2018).
4. **Fundamentals over hype, stated as a tripled chant.** His most-repeated critique of the industry compresses into a three-beat rhetorical structure: "As an industry we've been pushing: Automate. Automate. Automate. We should have been saying: Understand. Understand. Understand. Because if you understand what you're doing, you can automate if you want to" (tweet, Apr 17, 2022, reprinted at [changelog.com/posts/automation-is-the-serialization-of-understanding](https://changelog.com/posts/automation-is-the-serialization-of-understanding)).
5. **Aphorisms built for the timeline, not the page.** His tweets read as compressed parables rather than one-liners: "Maintaining an open-source project is like being a Flight Attendant for an airline where all tickets are free and the majority of customer surveys offer suggestions on how to fly the airplane," followed the same day by a second tweet delivering the punchline separately: "Landing is only available in the Enterprise version" (tweets, Jan 30, 2018, archived at [kelsey-hightower-wisdom](https://github.com/tgogos/kelsey-hightower-wisdom)). Elsewhere he coins a mock dictionary entry in exactly the register of a satirical glossary: "**service mess** /ˈsərvəs mes/ *noun* 1. the result of spending more compute resources than your actual business logic dynamically generating and distributing Envoy proxy configs and TLS certificates" (tweet, Jul 14, 2019, same archive).
6. **Empathy is engineered into the process, not just claimed.** Describing why representation at conferences matters, he doesn't state a value — he narrates a specific hallway encounter and lets the mechanism speak: "sometimes just showing up and being invisible, you give permission to all the people who thought they were not welcome. They realize that they can buy a ticket — because people that look like them are going to be there" (*Kelsey Hightower — Present*, 2023, recounting a conversation at KubeCon London).
7. **Personal material is deployed as teaching material, not confession.** He tells an extended, self-mocking story about buying a Suburban he didn't need — "I bought this Suburban. Car nut, right? No kids, no family, nothing. Just me, 5'9", and I have a seven-seater car... I had the car so you could see it, and be like 'Damn, he drives a Suburban.' That was why I had the car, literally. Just being honest with myself" (Changelog Interviews #595, official transcript) — and then generalizes it into an economic argument about debt and pricing, never lingering in the anecdote for its own sake.
8. **Saying no on purpose, and narrating the "no" as the lesson.** On declining to add a feature to his own open-source project, `confd`, and watching HashiCorp build a competitor instead: "The people at HashiCorp... there was a request to add Vault support to Confd... and I said no... And I celebrated Mitchell Hashimoto in the HashiCorp team for taking the idea of Confd and allowing it to live on in this way... And that's the power of open source that we can actually collaborate even when we're competing" (*Kelsey Hightower — Present*, 2023).

## Structure and formatting

Written pieces open with a one-sentence mission statement and never build up to it — the `kubernetes-the-hard-way` README's first line is its thesis: "This tutorial walks you through setting up Kubernetes the hard way." There is no cold open, no scene-setting, no hook in the essayistic sense; the hook is the plainness itself. Instructional material is staged as numbered, sequential "labs" (`01-prerequisites.md` through `13-cleanup.md`), each one opening with an identical low-key formula — "In this lab you will review the machine requirements necessary to follow this tutorial" — so that the structure itself teaches: same shape, every time, so the reader's attention goes to the content, not the format. Talks are staged the same way conceptually: rather than explain the Kubernetes scheduler's bin-packing behavior, he played an actual game of Tetris on stage at GopherCon 2016 to make the concept physically visible (widely documented, e.g. via [Rich Burroughs's recap](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/containers-tetris-rich-burroughs)) — the demo *is* the argument, not an illustration of one made elsewhere. Written pieces end abruptly, often on a one-line non sequitur rather than a summary: the `nocode` README's entire "Contributing" section reads "You don't." Spoken pieces, by contrast, rarely resolve into a tidy kicker — they trail into the next question, or land on a plain statement of fact rather than a rhetorical flourish, e.g., closing the maintainer-empathy passage in the ReadME Podcast with "I think that's the right way to participate in any open source community" and simply moving to the next question. The same minimalism governs supporting artifacts, not just headline READMEs: the demo script repo for his Monitorama PDX 2016 talk, "healthz," is nothing but a title, a one-line description ("Demo script for Monitorama 2016 talk"), and five bare `kubectl create -f` commands — no narration, no setup prose beyond a link to a separate setup doc (github.com/kelseyhightower/monitorama).

## Reference palette

Fast food and hourly-wage work (McDonald's, described as the pre-tech baseline of "survival"); home improvement and skilled trades performed by himself (running 20-amp electrical circuits for a bidet, reframing a fireplace, low-voltage cable work); cars and consumer debt as a lens on economics (the Suburban story, "things are not priced at what people can afford. Things are priced at what people can borrow," Changelog #595); open-source maintainer culture as its own small society (FOSDEM, the "different companies, same team" idea, the HashiCorp/Confd story); stage magic ("when you see a magician do a magic trick and they show you how they did it, it's not magic anymore"); aviation (the flight-attendant tweet; "the pilot falling asleep at the wheel" image for opaque automation, ReadME Podcast); monarchy and political economy as a mock-serious frame for discussing why people work ("we probably had kings and queens, manifest destiny," Changelog #595); and video games used literally rather than as pop-culture flavor — Tetris on stage as a working model of the Kubernetes scheduler, not a passing reference to it.

## Sentence-level habits

- **Plain, short declaratives in written work; long unspooling run-ons in spoken work.** The README register almost never exceeds one clause per sentence ("Yep. That's it."); the podcast register produces sentences that chain six or seven clauses with "and," "so," and "right?" before landing.
- **Self-interruption and self-correction mid-thought**, always spoken, never written: "I mean, I think if you've ever tried to maintain your own open source project, hopefully you grow a sense of empathy" — the "I mean" recurs as an opening hedge dozens of times across the transcripts, softening a claim before he commits to it.
- **Rhetorical questions posed to himself and then answered immediately**, functioning as a thinking-out-loud device rather than a reader hook: "And so what is work? And I think that's the real question... What is work, and what type of work do you want to do?" (Changelog Interviews #595).
- **A recurring "right?" as a spoken comma**, checking that the listener is still tracking the logic before he continues — dozens of instances across every transcript.
- **Coined, portable phrases that he reuses verbatim across different venues** rather than one-off wordplay: "different companies, same team," "learning in public," and "some people have ideas and some ideas have people" all recur across multiple interviews years apart, functioning like a settled personal lexicon rather than improvised wit.
- **Understatement instead of hype** when describing his own achievements: the entire origin of `kubernetes-the-hard-way` — one of the most-forked repos in the ecosystem — is summarized as "I decided that we didn't need more software. We needed some docs."
- **Profanity is rare and mild, and never used for aggression or emphasis** — the strongest language in the verified corpus is a stray "What the hell is this?" (Changelog & Friends #6) in the middle of a Westworld anecdote, not a rhetorical device; there are no insults and no genre-typical snark anywhere in the corpus.

## Signature moves

1. **The empty-code-block joke.** In `nocode`, the punchline is a literal absence: an empty fenced code block stands in for the "application," repeated for build, deploy, and scale steps, with only "The possibilities are endless" as connective tissue (`nocode` README).
2. **Teach the concept by physically performing it.** Rather than describe bin-packing, he plays Tetris live on stage at GopherCon 2016 so the scheduler's constraint-satisfaction problem is something the audience watches happen, not something they're told about.
3. **The obsolescence reversal.** Whenever asked to defend the importance of his own signature technology, he reframes success as the technology's disappearance: "if it goes away, that's a sign of progress" (*Kelsey Hightower — Present*, 2023).
4. **The portable coined phrase, reused as a settled belief rather than a fresh line.** "Different companies, same team" and "some people have ideas and some ideas have people" both appear as load-bearing ideas he returns to, not as one-time flourishes — the opposite of a spent-construction ledger; he treats his best lines as reusable infrastructure.
5. **Turning a personal anecdote into an economic or organizational argument, then dropping the anecdote.** The Suburban story ends not with a reflection on himself but with a generalized claim about how debt-based pricing works in the broader economy — the "I" is a specimen, not the point.
6. **Refusing individual credit in favor of the collective.** Closing the KubeCon + CloudNativeCon NA 2019 keynote "Reflections," he flattens his own outsized role in Kubernetes deliberately: "all of us are just pace setters... it's not about where you rank on the contribution list... it's not a race if we're all on the same team" (KubeCon NA 2019 keynote, [video](https://youtu.be/jiaLsxjBeOQ?t=721)).

## What NOT to do (what Hightower never does)

- **No jargon flexing.** Even talking to fellow engineers about CRDs, extension points, and the Kubernetes API model, he defines terms in plain language before using them rather than assuming shared vocabulary as a credential.
- **No hype language.** Nothing in the verified corpus resembles "game-changing," "revolutionary," or exclamation-point enthusiasm about his own work; the strongest praise he gives anything is functional ("it works," "that's amazing" reserved for other people's ideas, not his own).
- **No credential-flexing or résumé recitation.** He mentions Google, Distinguished Engineer status, or his own fame only when a host asks directly, and answers in a single deflecting clause before returning to the idea.
- **No condescension toward beginners.** The entire ethos of `kubernetes-the-hard-way` and the "learning in public" framing is the opposite of gatekeeping; he explicitly criticizes job postings that "need 50 years of experience for like 100 different things" as an artificial gate.
- **No venting, no blame-casting, and only the mildest possible profanity.** Even describing burnout, a public and painful HashiCorp/Confd dispute, or leaving Google, the register stays even and diagnostic rather than aggrieved.
- **No metaphor for its own sake.** Analogies (Tetris, the magician, the flight attendant) are always load-bearing explanations of a mechanism, never decorative flourishes dropped in for color.
- **No listicle formatting, no clickbait titles, no "10 things" structure.** Even his aphoristic tweets are single declarative thoughts, not numbered tips.

## How this differs from adjacent writers

Where a lot of cloud/devrel writing (Corey Quinn's *Last Week in AWS*, for instance) runs on snark, brand-needling, and rapid-fire jokes at vendors' expense, Hightower's humor is structural and deadpan rather than aggressive — the joke in `nocode` is an absence, not a jab at anyone. Where combative technical writers (a DHH, say) build arguments through provocation and score-settling, Hightower almost never names an adversary; his sharpest critiques ("we've been pushing Automate, Automate, Automate" instead of understanding) indict an industry-wide habit, not a company or a person. And where observability-and-SRE voices like Charity Majors write with more overt fire and profanity-adjacent bluntness about production pain, Hightower's bluntness is quieter and more procedural — he says no, explains why, and moves on, rather than making the refusal itself the rhetorical centerpiece.
