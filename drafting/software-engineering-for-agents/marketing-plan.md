# The Vibe Engineer’s Primer marketing plan

Build an audience of developers using AI coding tools by showing how the lessons of great software engineers apply to agentic coding. Develop the material once, then adapt it into articles, videos, talks, a book, and a course.

The audience will be primarily monetized through consulting engagements under the school of ["give 90% of what you know away for free, charge for the best 10%"](#appendix-a-giving-expertise-away-for-free). Any monies generated from content marketing are secondary.

The strategic opening is LinkedIn’s investment in video. LinkedIn is pushing traffic to accounts that post videos and the audience of vibe coders is large and growing.

The bet is that useful engineering lessons, sliced into hundreds of short videos with one topic apiece, can reach a receptive professional audience. A friend’s 45 short videos produced reported follower counts of 1,700 on LinkedIn, 1,600 on TikTok, and 700 on Instagram: encouraging evidence for trying this approach. [Research and context](research/marketing.md).

1. **Write the articles.** Publish substantive blog posts that can later become book chapters.
2. **Slice them into videos.** Turn each article into 2–10 short videos, each teaching one complete idea. Prioritize LinkedIn and adapt the clips for TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube.
3. **Build a speaking portfolio.** Use the published material, video examples, and audience response to pitch conference talks.
4. **Self-publish the book.** Edit and assemble the articles into a coherent primer, using reader feedback to improve the material.
5. **Pitch publishers.** Use the demonstrated audience and the self-published book’s performance to support a publishing proposal.
6. **Build the course.** Organize the videos into a learning sequence, adding explanations and exercises where needed.
7. **Optionally launch a podcast.** Interview authors whose work appears in the primer, exploring how their ideas apply to coding with agents.

Give interested viewers a clear path to the articles and email subscription. Track follower growth by platform, substantive engagement, subscribers, speaking opportunities, and interest in the book and course.

## Appendix A: Giving Expertise Away for Free

Three references for sharing substantial free content to build an audience and a business. These are practitioner positions, not evidence that a particular percentage is optimal for consulting. The original source of the remembered “80–90% of your knowledge” advice remains unidentified. Verification and context are recorded in [marketing research, M04–M06](research/marketing.md#m04-john-sonmez-free-and-paid-content).

### John Sonmez: 90% free, 10% paid

In [The McMethod Email Marketing Podcast, episode 106](https://www.dropdeadcopy.com/episode-106-john-sonmez-on-being-a-rockstar-in-your-niche-and-beyond/) (2015), Sonmez discusses building a reputation through blogs, podcasts, and other content:

> I give away 90% of what I do for free and I charge for 10% of it.

He repeats the rule in [How to Build a Personal Brand and Reputation as a Software Developer](https://simpleprogrammer.com/personal-brand-reputation/). This is the clearest match for the free/paid split.

### Ramit Sethi: Free material that earns trust

On [Being Boss, episode 57](https://beingboss.club/podcast/podcast-episode-57-i-will-teach-you-to-be-rich-ramit-sethi) (February 2, 2016), at **59:24**, Sethi says:

> Yeah, we give away 90% of our stuff.

He explains that his goal is for the free material to outperform other people’s paid material, giving readers a chance to judge its value before buying.

### Joe Pulizzi: Make content freely accessible

In a [May 7, 2013 interview with Adam Franklin](https://www.bluewiremedia.com.au/how-to-do-content-marketing-like-the-godfather-joe-pulizzi/), under “Give 90% away free,” Pulizzi recommends:

> well over 90% of that content ungated, freely accessible content

His percentage concerns access without registration forms. It supports broad distribution, but does not specify how much knowledge to reserve for paying clients.

## Appendix B: The Opportunity in Online Course Sales

Matt Pocock’s Total TypeScript demonstrates the financial upside of a focused course for working developers: millions of dollars in reported sales from teaching one programming language. It gives this plan a concrete example of how course revenue could become substantial while consulting remains the primary objective. Sources and limits are recorded in [marketing research, M07–M10](research/marketing.md#m07-matt-pocock-on-the-pragmatic-engineer).

### From free teaching to a course business

In [The Pragmatic Engineer’s September 17, 2026 interview](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ai-skills-with-matt-pocock), Pocock describes returning to teaching after an earlier career teaching voice and singing. Short TypeScript tips attracted an audience. He worked three days a week for Vercel while developing the course, then left when sales took off.

The first commercial test was a live workshop. Joel Hooks’s [December 2022 launch account](https://badass.dev/launch-of-a-developer-education-product) reports that all 30 seats sold, priced at $1,200 with a $900 early-bird offer. The workshop tested willingness to pay before the recorded product was complete.

### Reported financial results

| Milestone | Reported result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| December 2022 pre-release | $415,000 in gross sales; more than 1,200 learners | [Hooks’s launch account](https://badass.dev/launch-of-a-developer-education-product) |
| October 2024 interview | $2.5 million in course sales | [Cory House’s interview with Pocock](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XDhqcOlCDf8) |

The 2026 Pragmatic Engineer episode describes more than $2.5 million in total sales. These are cumulative business sales, not annual income or Pocock’s take-home earnings; the pre-release is part of the course’s sales history, not an additional amount to add to the later total. Pocock shared revenue with his publishing partner and incurred expenses. These are participant-reported figures, not audited accounts.

Pocock describes the outcome as life-changing: course sales could continue while he stepped away to spend time with his family. The [supplied interview transcript](research/mattpocock_interview_pragmatic_engineer.md) captures this account, with transcription-quality limits recorded in M07.

### What made the offer valuable

In [their August 2023 retrospective](https://badass.dev/creating-total-typescript-with-matt-pocock), Pocock and Hooks describe serving experienced developers with advanced material and recognizing employer training budgets as a buying opportunity. Their partnership combined teaching with instructional design and the business of selling courses.

In the [2024 House interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XDhqcOlCDf8), Pocock explains that live workshops tested the material before recording. The course used short problem-and-solution videos, and his partners handled support and email campaigns. Free teaching attracted learners; the paid product offered an organized learning path.

For the Primer, this suggests testing demand for a coherent sequence of practical exercises alongside the free articles and clips. A paid workshop could test that demand before investing in the full course. This is an application of the case, not a revenue forecast.

Pocock also reports falling Total TypeScript revenue in the [2026 interview](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ai-skills-with-matt-pocock), attributing it to AI reducing demand for readily available knowledge. His emphasis has shifted toward judgment and strategic work. He also argues for the value of human curation: arranging prerequisite knowledge into a sensible learning sequence. That supports exploring the Primer’s engineering focus, while leaving buyers’ willingness to pay to be tested.
