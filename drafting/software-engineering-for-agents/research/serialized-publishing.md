# Serialized publishing: the *Wool* model and its fit for the primer

Checked 2026-10-13. Internal planning reference, not post prose. Marshall asked for research on publishing the primer chapter by chapter, the way Hugh Howey published *Wool* ([D33](../DECISIONS.md#d33-serialized-publishing-research)). This report explains how the *Wool* serial worked, sets it beside the serialized and early-access models used for technical books, records the platform rules that constrain each one, and closes with an editorial assessment of the options. Nothing here selects a publishing strategy.

## Evidence status

The first pass ran without page access and relied on search summaries. With network access restored, every entry was then checked against its cited pages on 2026-10-13, and corrected where the pages disagreed. Entries are tiered as **verified primary** (the originator's own page, read), **secondary** (press or third-party reporting, read) or **folklore** (anecdote or unsourced). The few claims that still couldn't be read are marked unverified in place and listed under [Still unverified](#still-unverified). Quotations are verbatim from the fetched pages.

## Summary

- ***Wool* was not planned as a serial.** Howey released a standalone 11,000-word story for $0.99 on 2011-07-30. Reader demand pulled four more parts out of him between late November 2011 and late January 2012, and the omnibus released alongside part 5 became the main seller (W2–W5).
- **The mechanics were a feedback loop, not a release plan.** A complete first part, four sequels in about eight weeks started in response to reader email and reviews, each part longer than the last, and a bundled edition priced below the parts combined that paid Howey the same per reader (W3–W6). A year in, the first part became a permanent free sample (W6).
- **The audience became leverage.** Self-published e-book income let Howey turn down seven-figure offers that wanted e-book rights, and take a print-only deal with Simon & Schuster instead (W10, W11). This maps onto steps 4 and 5 of the [marketing plan](../marketing-plan.md): self-publish, then pitch publishers from a demonstrated audience.
- **The primer differs from *Wool* in two ways that matter.** Its chapters will be free on the blog first, and its subject changes fast. *Wool*'s parts were paid and its text was finished once. Andy Weir's free-then-$0.99 *The Martian* (T10) and Will Larson's free *StaffEng* site plus paid book (T6) are closer precedents for free-first. For the publisher step, Larson drafted *An Elegant Puzzle*'s chapters as blog posts before Stripe Press bought the finished manuscript (T16).
- **Platform rules shape the choice.** Free blog chapters rule out KDP Select and Kindle Unlimited (P4). KDP now delivers revised editions to earlier buyers, but a large expansion may need a new edition (P7); Leanpub is built for a book that grows, with every update free to every buyer (T1). KDP also requires a serialized part to say so and bars installments that send readers elsewhere for the rest (P3, P5). Kindle Vella, Amazon's serial platform, closed on 2025-02-26 (P13).
- **Blog posts and chapters can drift apart.** Gergely Orosz says releasing book drafts as newsletter issues delayed his book by two years (T8). Larson avoided it by outlining first (T16).

## Part 1: How *Wool* worked

Checked against the cited pages on 2026-10-13. Howey's own blog posts carry most of the timeline. The WSJ original (Alexandra Alter, ["Sci-Fi's Underground Hit"](https://www.wsj.com/articles/SB10001424127887324678604578340752088305668), 2013-03-07) is paywalled and was not read.

### W1. Before *Wool*

**Secondary (strong).** Howey's first novel, *Molly Fyde and the Parsona Rescue*, came out from a small press, Norlights Press, in 2009: "Norlights Press, in Indiana, had released his YA novel Molly Fyde and the Parsona Rescue in 2009" ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20120409/51416-self-made-bestseller-weighs-traditional-deals.html), 2012-04-06). He self-published his later books, including later editions of *Molly Fyde*.

### W2. The standalone first part

**Verified primary.** Howey uploaded the original *Wool* on 2011-07-30 ([Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/), 2013-07-28). "The first WOOL was around 11,000 words. … I priced it at the minimum and never figured it would sell more than a few copies" ([JanNoWriMo and Pricing Woes](https://hughhowey.com/jannowrimo-and-pricing-woes/), 2012-01-15). He didn't promote it: "No publicity, no option to buy a copy on my website, no tweets" ([Maybe I should finally mention WOOL](https://hughhowey.com/maybe-i-should-finally-mention-wool/), 2011-11-08). Publishers Weekly says he first released it "through multiple platforms", not only on Kindle.

**Secondary.** He wrote it partly on lunch breaks while working at Appalachian State University's bookstore ([College of Charleston](https://today.cofc.edu/2013/06/19/shattered-worlds/), 2013-06-19).

### W3. Readers asked for more

**Verified primary.** "By the end of October, the story had made it on the Kindle sci-fi best-seller list. Howey started to get emails from readers asking him for the rest of the story" ([Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/books/2013/03/hugh_howey_and_wool_how_the_self_pubbed_sci_fi_writer_relates_to_fans.html), 2013-03-01). Howey: "I thought the first book was all I was ever going to write" ([Charleston Magazine](https://mail.charlestonmag.com/node/30010), 2013-03-21). By November 2011, "I had over a thousand readers, and many of them were writing reviews and emails begging for the next entry. On November 1st … I started writing and outlining the rest of the series" ([Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/)).

Only the first part was unplanned. Once he committed to continuing, he planned the serial on purpose: "Yes, I have a plan. A carefully constructed one" ([Wool 2 – Proper Gauge](https://hughhowey.com/wool-2-proper-gauge/), 2011-12-02).

### W4. Parts 2–5 and their cadence

**Verified primary.** The four sequels came out over about eight weeks, each longer than the one before.

| Part | Release | Length | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. *Wool* | 2011-07-30 | ~11,000 words | $0.99 |
| 2. *Proper Gauge* | About 2011-11-30 | ~20,000 words | $0.99 |
| 3. *Casting Off* | 2011-12-04 | ~30,000 words | $0.99 |
| 4. *The Unraveling* | Just before Christmas 2011 | ~40,000 words | $0.99 |
| 5. *The Stranded* | 2012-01-26 | 57,000+ words | $2.99 |

Sources: [Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/); [JanNoWriMo and Pricing Woes](https://hughhowey.com/jannowrimo-and-pricing-woes/); [Wool 5 is go for launch](https://hughhowey.com/wool-5-is-go-for-launch/) (2012-01-22); [Number Five is ALIVE!!](https://hughhowey.com/number-five-is-alive/) (2012-01-26).

The first part kept selling while the sequels came out: 1,018 copies in October 2011, "3,000 copies of Wool in November", and "10,000 copies of Wool. Thousands of copies of Wool 2" in December ([Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/)). Howey later named the escalation as a design choice: "It started short and slow, and then each sequel got a little longer while picking up the pace" ([A wild first week](https://hughhowey.com/a-wild-first-week-as-a-writer/), 2012-02-03).

### W5. The omnibus

**Verified primary for date and price.** The *Wool Omnibus* (parts 1–5) went on sale on 2012-01-26, the same day as part 5, at $4.99 ([Let's do the math](https://hughhowey.com/lets-do-the-math/), 2012-01-26).

**Secondary for sales.** It sold about 23,000 copies within a month ([College of Charleston](https://today.cofc.edu/2013/06/19/shattered-worlds/)). Its New York Times run came in summer 2012, not at launch. After a Gizmodo mention in July 2012, "The Omnibus went into the top 10 on all of Amazon and cracked the New York Times bestseller chart" ([Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/); also [Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/books/2013/03/hugh_howey_and_wool_how_the_self_pubbed_sci_fi_writer_relates_to_fans.html)). Writer's Digest and the College of Charleston put the list run at two weeks. Howey: "I watched as the five individual books slid down the rankings and the Omnibus rose up above them."

### W6. The pricing arithmetic

**Verified primary.** The five parts cost $6.95 separately, and the omnibus cost $4.99, yet Howey earned the same either way. The four $0.99 parts paid 35% (35 cents each) and part 5 paid 70% ($2.09), while the $4.99 omnibus paid 70%: "$4.99 * .70 = $3.49 / Exact same amount of money to me" ([Let's do the math](https://hughhowey.com/lets-do-the-math/)). He later raised the omnibus to $5.99 "due to complaints from readers that my books were too cheap!" In July 2012 he made *Wool* 1 permanently free ([Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/)).

### W7. Income

**Secondary**, except where Howey speaks for himself. These figures come from different outlets and dates. Cite each with its source rather than as a steady rate.

| Period | Reported figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Jan–Mar 2012 | "$19,000 from his fiction in January, $50,000 in February, and $70,000 in March" | [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20120409/51416-self-made-bestseller-weighs-traditional-deals.html), 2012-04-06 |
| Summer 2012 | "selling 20,000–30,000 digital copies of Wool a month … and making a monthly salary of $150,000 from e-book sales alone" | [Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/be-inspired/how-hugh-howey-turned-his-self-published-story-wool-into-a-success-a-book-deal) |
| 2012–13 | About $120,000 a month. Howey: "I had some months better than that, but that's about an average for the last year" | [Meet Innovators](https://meetinnovators.com/2013/08/12/hugh-howey-author-wool/), 2013-08-12 |
| 2012 | "more than $1 million through digital sales of Wool in 2012" | [Charleston Magazine](https://mail.charlestonmag.com/node/30010), 2013-03-21 |

### W8. He repeated the pattern

**Verified primary.** Howey serialized again with the *Shift* prequels. *First Shift* came out on 2012-04-14 ([Okay, here's the thing](https://hughhowey.com/okay-heres-the-thing/)) and *Second Shift* in the second half of 2012. *Third Shift* and the *Shift Omnibus* went up together in late January 2013 ([Book-Books](https://hughhowey.com/book-books/), 2013-01-28). He wrote *Dust* (2013) as a single novel, for the reader's sake: "In fairness to the reader, I think it's going to be nice to give them the entire work so they don't have to wait in between pieces" ([Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/arts/books/2013/03/hugh_howey_and_wool_how_the_self_pubbed_sci_fi_writer_relates_to_fans.html)). That is the closest thing found to a reservation about serializing.

### W9. Film rights

**Secondary (strong).** "Self-publishing success Hugh Howey has sold film rights to his science fiction series Wool to 20th Century Fox," partnering with Ridley and Tony Scott's Scott Free and Steve Zaillian's Film Rites ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/page-to-screen/article/51934-hugh-howey-s-wool-nabbed-by-20th-century-fox.html), 2012-05-14). Howey later described it as an option ([Meet Innovators](https://meetinnovators.com/2013/08/12/hugh-howey-author-wool/)). The film was never made.

### W10. The print-only deal

**Secondary.** "Hugh Howey has made a print-only deal with Simon & Schuster, which will release his title in both hardcover and paperback editions simultaneously in March 2013 while Howey continues to control the ebook version." Print-only deals were "rare, though not singular"; Mira had licensed print-only rights from Bella Andre that September ([Publishers Lunch](https://lunch.publishersmarketplace.com/2012/12/wool-author-makes-print-only-deal-with-simon-schuster/), Michael Cader, 2012-12-12). Wikipedia, citing the WSJ, puts the deal at mid-six figures.

**Verified primary.** Howey describes the deal as "a print-only deal that would revert even those rights after a set period of time. … All it took was walking away from a few 7-figure deals" ([Two Years Ago](https://hughhowey.com/two-years-ago/)).

### W11. Why keeping e-book rights mattered

**Verified primary.** "It's hard to think about giving up a 70% royalty for an 18% royalty" ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20120409/51416-self-made-bestseller-weighs-traditional-deals.html), April 2012). Elsewhere: "You also make 70% of your ebook's price rather than 15%" ([A Question from Calvin](https://hughhowey.com/a-question-from-calvin/), 2012-08-19). His digital rights alone "were more lucrative than what I've been offered for my print rights and my digital rights" ([Meet Innovators](https://meetinnovators.com/2013/08/12/hugh-howey-author-wool/)). His percentages vary by source; cite his own wording rather than a standard contract rate.

### W12. Foreign rights and television

**Secondary.** UK rights went to Century, a Random House division, after a five-way auction at the London Book Fair ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/self-published-sf-author-hugh-51420121), 2012-05-14). By December 2012, "agent Kristin Nelson and her sub-agents have already licensed the book in over 18 territories" ([Publishers Lunch](https://lunch.publishersmarketplace.com/2012/12/wool-author-makes-print-only-deal-with-simon-schuster/)).

**Verified primary.** Apple TV+'s *Silo* premiered on 2023-05-05. Graham Yost created it and is showrunner, Rebecca Ferguson stars, and Howey is an executive producer. It is "based on Hugh Howey's New York Times bestselling trilogy" ([Apple press release](https://www.apple.com/tv-pr/news/2023/03/apple-tv-unearths-first-look-at-gripping-new-series-silo-and-sets-global-premiere-for-friday-may-5-2023/), 2023-03-06).

### W13. Later advocacy

**Verified primary.** On 2014-02-11, Howey and a programmer partner launched AuthorEarnings.com with a first report estimating self-published authors' share of Amazon e-book sales from bestseller-list data ([AuthorEarnings.com](https://hughhowey.com/authorearnings-com/)). That domain now serves unrelated spam.

**Secondary.** Critics disputed the method: "Many people have questioned the methodology in his previous reports" (James M. Jackson, [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6721292-author-earnings-3rd-quarter-report-making-an-iceberg-from-an-ice-cube), 2014-07-27).

### What transfers from *Wool*

These are editorial readings of W1–W13, not findings.

1. **A complete first installment.** *Wool* 1 worked on its own, and the series grew out of demand. For a primer, that means each released chapter teaches a whole idea, which the marketing plan already requires of each article and video.
2. **Readers set the sequence.** Howey started the sequels in response to reader email and reviews. The primer's 113-lesson menu allows the same move: publish early chapters, watch which ideas draw the strongest response, and pick the next chapters accordingly.
3. **Short gaps while attention is high.** Four sequels arrived in about eight weeks. Howey credited the serial with "reduced wait between reads" ([Let's do the math](https://hughhowey.com/lets-do-the-math/)), and later wrote *Dust* whole so readers wouldn't have to wait between pieces (W8). A skeptic, Claude Forthomme, argues instead that serials fail because readers feel short-changed by partial books, and calls *Wool* a "black swan". She explicitly rejects long gaps as the cause ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4943661-the-verdict-is-in-serializing-your-novel-does-not-work), 2013-09-27).
4. **The bundle captures readers who wait.** The omnibus overtook the individual parts (W5). Its price was set so Howey earned the same whichever way a reader bought (W6).
5. **The first part became the free sample.** A year in, Howey made *Wool* 1 permanently free (W6). That is the free-first-installment model the primer's blog would start from.
6. **The audience becomes leverage.** Howey negotiated from proven sales and kept the rights that earned the most (W10, W11).

What doesn't transfer: fiction's cliffhangers, and the single fixed text. A book about agentic coding will need revision as tools change, and *Wool* never did.

### Myths and do-not-use

- **"First self-published author offered a print-only deal by a major publisher."** Writer's Digest and the College of Charleston both say this. Publishers Lunch says such deals were rare, with an earlier example (W10). Say "rare."
- **"$150,000 a month" or "$120,000 a month" as a steady income.** The figures come from different periods, and Howey calls $120,000 an average (W7).
- **"*Wool* was planned as a serial."** The first part was a standalone. From part 2 on, the serial was deliberate (W3).
- **"Seven-figure Simon & Schuster deal."** The seven-figure offers were the ones he walked away from (W10).
- **"Ridley Scott made a *Wool* film."** It was optioned and never made; the adaptation is the Apple TV+ series (W9, W12).
- **"Serializing your novel does not work" attributed to Howey.** Claude Forthomme wrote it.
- **"12,000 words" for *Wool* 1.** Howey says around 11,000.
- **"Parts 2–5 came out October–December 2011."** They ran from about 2011-11-30 to 2012-01-26 (W4).
- **"Part 2 sold 3,000 a month; parts 3–4 sold 10,000."** Those were Howey's November and December 2011 sales of *Wool* 1 (W4).
- **"Every installment cost 99 cents."** Part 5 was $2.99 (W4).
- **SF Encyclopedia's dates for the *Shift* parts and The Loop's (2019) account of the Fox deal.** Both are wrong; don't cite either for dates.
- **The author's note in *The Stranded*** as a source for the series being unplanned. Its only citation, RealClearBooks, is dead; use Howey's own posts (W3).

## Part 2: Serialized and early-access models for technical books

Checked against the cited pages on 2026-10-13. Items that couldn't be read are marked.

### T1. Leanpub in-progress books

**Verified primary.** Leanpub sells ebooks "in progress and completed", and "authors can publish updated versions of their ebooks and courses for all of their readers with a click" ([About Leanpub](https://leanpub.com/about)). Authors set a minimum price, which can be free, and a suggested price, and buyers choose what to pay ([variable pricing](https://help.leanpub.com/en/articles/110750-why-do-i-see-a-suggested-price-and-a-minimum-price-when-i-buy-a-leanpub-book-what-is-variable-pricing)). Leanpub recommends "starting low and increasing it over time so your early adopters don't feel taken advantage of", and reports that "about a third of people will pay the suggested price, a third will pay the minimum price and a third will pay somewhere in between" ([raising price over time](https://help.leanpub.com/en/articles/110704-can-i-change-the-price-of-my-book-after-publishing-it-for-example-can-i-start-cheap-release-new-chapters-over-time-and-increase-the-price-as-my-book-s-getting-more-valuable)). That split is Leanpub's own observation, not data. Royalty terms are in P9.

### T2. Jeff Geerling's *Ansible for DevOps*

**Verified primary.** Before finishing the book, Geerling reported that it "has netted me over $25,000 in sales (so far)" on more than 2,000 in-progress copies ([2015 post](https://www.jeffgeerling.com/blog/2015/25k-book-sales-and-im-almost-ready-publish/), 2015-09-18). By 2023 it had "sold 87,234 copies as of this writing across LeanPub, Amazon (Kindle and paperback), and iBooks" and "earned $300,000+ in revenue over the past 9 years", across 41 revisions ([2023 post](https://www.jeffgeerling.com/blog/2023/self-publishing-technical-book-10-years/), 2023-06-29). Once he listed a complete edition on Amazon, "sales exploded—and have not let up", though Leanpub stayed "a reliable source of sales". Existing blog posts seeded his first three chapters, and he gives Leanpub buyers "free updates forever". This is the strongest technical-book precedent for selling a book while writing it and updating it for years.

### T3. Manning MEAP

**Verified primary.** "In MEAP, you read a book chapter-by-chapter while it's being written and get the final eBook as soon as it's finished." Each MEAP book gets a liveBook discussion forum, and "Authors and Editors check the forum for suggestions and use the feedback to improve the final draft." MEAP began in 2003 and is sold only on manning.com. [Manning MEAP](https://www.manning.com/meap-program).

### T4. Pragmatic Bookshelf beta books

**Verified primary.** A beta book ships "in its current state as an eBook", which "could be as little as 50% complete". Buyers are emailed when it updates and get "free updates for the lifetime of that edition of the book". Each book's page links to an errata form, and every PDF page carries a "Report Erratum" link. At the beta stage PragProg wants to hear about "technical errors and missing or hard-to-understand text". [PragProg support](https://pragprog.com/support/#beta-books). PragProg says it "pioneered the 'beta book' concept" ([About](https://pragprog.com/about/)).

### T5. O'Reilly Early Release

**Secondary for the past model; verified primary for the current one.** O'Reilly once sold raw, unedited early-access ebooks whose buyers got "updates as I add more chapters and revise the existing ones" and then the final ebook ([Kief Morris](https://infrastructure-as-code.com/posts/early-release-is-available.html), 2015-03-13). Today O'Reilly lists "early release books" as included in its subscription learning platform ([pricing](https://www.oreilly.com/online-learning/pricing)), so readers reach them through a subscription rather than buying a book.

### T6. Will Larson's *Staff Engineer*

**Verified primary.** "The vast majority of the writing included in this book is available for free on staffeng.com, and will continue to available for free indefinitely"; only the foreword and ending are book-only ([staffeng.com/book](https://staffeng.com/book)). The paid digital Early Edition, "draftier" and still being reworked "thanks to user feedback", went on preorder on 2021-01-01 and shipped at the end of January, and "anyone who buys the Early Edition will get the final digital version as well" ([early edition post](https://lethain.com/staff-eng-early-edition/)). The final edition came out in early February 2021 at $25, on Gumroad and Amazon ([release post](https://lethain.com/staff-engineer/)). All profits go to non-profits, and no sales figures were published, so the case says nothing about author income. **This is the closest analogue to the primer's self-publishing plan:** free web chapters, a short paid early edition, then a packaged book.

### T7. Kent Beck's *Tidy First?*

**Verified primary.** Beck drafted *Tidy First?* on his newsletter before O'Reilly published it in 2023. "My initial purpose in offering paid subscriptions was a trade—I would get an incentive to keep writing & subscribers would get early access to material." It worked: he got the draft done and received feedback that refined the outline and chapters ([State of Tidy First?](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/state-of-tidy-first), 2023-07-31). His follow-up book stalled: "it became clear that the material in Tidy Together was being overtaken by events" ([Tidy Together Reboot](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/tidy-together-reboot), 2026-01-29; the reason is behind the paywall). This is paid early access, not free-first, and it shows a traditional technical publisher accepting material first drafted on a newsletter.

### T8. Gergely Orosz's *The Software Engineer's Guidebook*

**Verified primary.** Orosz pitched O'Reilly, the Pragmatic Bookshelf and Manning. Manning signed the book, but after three months "it was clear that the publisher and my book weren't a good match". One concern was the contract: "I … feared my hands could be tied if I wanted to share draft material online." He self-published, and the book took four years instead of the expected six to twelve months ([pitching post](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/four-years-on-writing-a-tech-book-pitching-to-a-publisher/), 2025-11-11). He reported "$611,911 in two years from royalties on 40,000 copies sold" ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-software-engineers-guidebook), 2025-11-11).

**The strongest caution for a blog-first book.** In 2021 Orosz started his newsletter planning "to send out a draft version of a part of the book as a newsletter every few weeks". Newsletter issues grew deeper and more time-bound than book chapters, and the plan "didn't hasten the publication of The Software Engineer's Guidebook. It actually delayed it by another two years" (pitching post).

### T9. Julia Evans's zines

**Verified primary.** Evans sold early access to *Let's learn tcpdump* for $10 before releasing it free: "190 people bought it in all, for a total of $1970" ([tcpdump zine](https://jvns.ca/blog/2017/05/14/learn-tcpdump-zine/), 2017-05-14). Her 2018 zine revenue was $101,558 ([2019 revenue](https://jvns.ca/blog/2019/10/01/zine-revenue-2019/)). In August 2019 No Starch Press published a box set of her *free* zines, *Your Linux Toolbox*, which had sold 4,000 copies by December ([2019 review](https://jvns.ca/blog/2019-year-in-review/)). Free material still sold as print.

### T10. Andy Weir's *The Martian*

**Secondary.** Weir spent three years "posting a new chapter every few weeks on his website, where readers could catch up … for free". At readers' request he made a free e-reader file, then a Kindle edition at 99 cents: "Over six months, 35,000 copies were sold, with Weir earning 30 cents on each one" ([Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/how-a-99-cent-novel-842582/), 2015). The Kindle edition's success led Crown to buy print rights, and its edition came out in February 2014 ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20150914/68033-the-martian-s-chronicle.html), 2015-09-14). Other sources say three months for the 35,000; THR says six.

**The free stage ended with the deals.** "At Podium's request, Weir removed the free version of the novel from his site and then, at Crown's request, the Kindle e-book." Crown also re-edited the book, changing the ending (Publishers Weekly). Fiction, but the closest free-first parallel to *Wool*: readers who could read for free paid for the convenient edition, and publishers then wanted the free copy gone.

### T11. Dickens's part-publication

**Secondary.** *The Pickwick Papers* came out in 20 parts, in 19 monthly issues from April 1836 to November 1837 (the last was a double), at a shilling each ([Wake Forest ZSR Library](https://zsr.wfu.edu/2012/the-posthumous-papers-of-the-pickwick-club-by-charles-dickens-1836-37/)). The first part's 1,000 copies were hard to sell and the second part's run was cut to 500, but "by the final part … sales reached 40,000 copies" ([History of Information](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=4403)). Readers paid for every part, so this is paid serialization, like *Wool*, not free-first.

### T12. Amazon's serial platforms

**Verified primary for the launch.** Amazon launched Kindle Serials on 2012-09-06, "enabling readers to buy a book once and follow as it's written, with future episodes added seamlessly at no additional cost". It was a curated Amazon Publishing imprint, not a self-serve KDP feature: authors submitted ideas, and eight titles launched at $1.99 ([Amazon press release](https://press.aboutamazon.com/2012/9/amazon-publishing-introduces-kindle-serials-great-stories-one-episode-at-a-time)).

**Folklore.** No end announcement was found; secondary sources say Amazon stopped adding serials around 2014. Kindle Vella is covered in P13.

### T13. Web serials and Substack

**Secondary.** Royal Road is "a (huge and free) site for web novels" that "also ties to Patreon, where writers can monetize their releases"; one author there posts a book on Patreon while still releasing chapters publicly ([Lyse Beck](https://litopia.com/lyse-beck/a-royal-road-adventure/)). Chuck Palahniuk serialized *Greener Pastures* on Substack behind a paywall, with his writing advice free ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/chuck-palahniuk-joins-substack-greener-pastures-novel/), 2021-09-20). Both sell access to posts, not a downloadable book.

### T14. Does free cannibalize paid?

**Verified primary for the abstract.** John Hilton III and David Wiley (2010) used BookScan data for 41 books in four categories, eight weeks before and after free digital versions appeared: "Three of the four categories of books had increased sales after the free books were distributed" ([BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/826); *Journal of Electronic Publishing* 13(1)). **Unverified:** that the exception was Tor's titles, which had been free for only a week behind a registration wall, and how many fell. The full text couldn't be read.

**Verified primary; argument, not data.** Tim O'Reilly, 2002: "Obscurity is a far greater threat to authors and creative artists than piracy" ([original](http://www.openp2p.com/pub/a/p2p/2002/12/11/piracy.html), 2002-12-11; [O'Reilly republication](https://www.oreilly.com/content/piracy-is-progressive-taxation-and-other-thoughts-on-the-evolution-of-online-distribution/)). Cory Doctorow released *Down and Out in the Magic Kingdom* (Tor, 2003) free under Creative Commons: "Three years and six printings later, more than 700,000 copies of the book have been downloaded from my site." He adds: "Most people who download the book don't end up buying it, but they wouldn't have bought it in any event" (his 2006 Forbes essay, quoted by [Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/2008/09/09/cory-doctorow-releases-content-selected-essays-on-technology-creativity-copyright-and-the-future-of-the-future/), 2008-09-09).

### T15. Publishers and previously blogged material

**Verified primary, cutting both ways.**

- **For.** Stripe Press bought Larson's *An Elegant Puzzle* after its chapters ran as blog posts (T16). O'Reilly published Beck's newsletter-drafted *Tidy First?* (T7). Geerling's blog posts seeded his self-published book (T2).
- **Against.** Orosz worried a publisher's contract could stop him sharing drafts online (T8). Crown had Weir take down his free web version (T10).

**Secondary; wording unverified.** Agent Chip MacGregor reportedly says many editors are uneasy about material that has already run on a blog, and advises holding some fresh content back. The [original post](https://macgregorandluedeke.com/blog/do-i-include-my-blog-experience-in-my-proposal) now returns a 404, and its archived copy couldn't be read.

Marshall owns the yountlabs.com posts, so the open question is a publisher's appetite and contract terms, not its legal ability to take them. The contract may matter more: Orosz's and Weir's cases both involved terms about free copies.

### T16. Will Larson's *An Elegant Puzzle*

**Verified primary.** Larson outlined the book first, then "wrote each of the chapters as a blog post, which let me get into the 'habit of shipping' … and to test the content with readers." Stripe Press "typically buy completed manuscripts, rather than proposals", and he finalized its interest after the content was written ([What I learned writing a book](https://lethain.com/learned-writing-book/), 2019-06-08). Stripe Press says the book "draws from writing on his blog, Irrational Exuberance" ([Stripe Press](https://press.stripe.com/an-elegant-puzzle)). Larson's book page gives 33,493 copies sold by January 2021 ([sketches](https://sketches.lethain.com/books/elegant-puzzle/)). **This is the closest precedent for the primer's blog-first-then-publisher path:** outline, then chapters as public posts, then a publisher buys the finished manuscript.

### Do-not-use

- **"Hilton & Wikström" study.** The study is Hilton & Wiley (2010).
- **Leanpub's lifetime payout totals.** Its pages give conflicting figures.
- **Royal Road top-earner Patreon incomes** and **promotional Substack serial-fiction figures.** Secondhand estimates and marketing copy.
- ***An Elegant Puzzle* as "a collection of revised blog posts."** Misleading: Larson outlined the book and drafted chapters as posts (T16).
- **"Orosz's book was not serialized."** He tried sending drafts out as newsletter issues and says it delayed the book (T8).
- **"20 of 24 Tor titles fell"** (Hilton & Wiley). Not verified against the full text.

## Part 3: Platform rules that constrain a serial

Each entry was checked against the platform's own page on 2026-10-13 and is verified primary unless marked otherwise. These pages change; refetch before relying on any figure.

### P1. KDP 70% band

**Verified primary.** "Effective July 7, 2026, the 70% royalty option price band is $2.99-$12.99 on Amazon.com, expanding from the previous range of $2.99-$9.99." The $9.99 cap had been in place since 2007. Delivery costs come out of 70% royalties, at $0.15/MB in the US. The list price must be "at least 20% below the list price on Amazon for any physical edition". Sales in Brazil, Japan, Mexico and India earn 70% only for books enrolled in KDP Select. [List price requirements](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200634560); [Digital Book Pricing Page](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200634500).

### P2. $0.99 installments earn 35%

**Verified primary.** $0.99 is the minimum list price at the 35% rate for files under 3 MB; the minimum rises to $1.99 at 3 MB and $2.99 at 10 MB, which matters for installments with images. KDP deducts no delivery cost at 35%. Amazon's own worked example gives $0.35 per $0.99 sale at 0% VAT. [List price requirements](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200634560); [eBook royalties](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200644210). This matches the roughly $0.30 per copy reported for Weir (T10).

### P3. Content freely available online

**Verified primary.** KDP "will not accept content under copyright that is freely available on the web unless it's provided by the owner of the copyright" ([content guidelines](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200672390)), so Marshall can publish his own blog chapters on KDP. The [content quality guide](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200952510)'s list of disappointing content includes three more items that bear on a serial:

- "Content that is either marketed as a subscription or redirects readers to an external source to obtain the full content". An installment can't send readers to the blog for the rest.
- "Content that is not significantly different from content in another book available in the Kindle Store" and "Content that is too short". Thin per-chapter installments are at risk.
- "Content that is excessively reused, recycled, or repeated within or across books". The same page's multi-work section allows collections of "content you've previously published in your catalog", so an omnibus of one's own installments is allowed if the reuse isn't excessive.

**Folklore.** Authors widely report that KDP sends rights-verification emails when a book's text appears online. Keep proof of authorship ready.

### P4. KDP Select rules out free blog chapters

**Verified primary.** Enrolling in KDP Select means "you cannot distribute your book digitally anywhere else, including on your website, blogs, etc." ([KDP Select](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/select)). The Select terms also bar distributing "a book that is substantially similar" in digital format ([KDP terms](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200627430)). Authors "may choose to make up to 10% of your book available on other sites as a sample" ([enrolling in Select](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GD9PMU58BV24QFZ7)). Terms run in 90-day periods. **Chapters that stay free on the blog rule out KDP Select and Kindle Unlimited**, unless the blog copies are cut to 10% or less. Selling on Leanpub at the same time would also breach Select.

### P5. Series pages, box sets and serial labeling

**Verified primary.** KDP series can be ordered or unordered ([reading order](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G29L44KLZ6RDYGDP)). A Kindle box set attaches to a series only "as related content", and KDP offers no paperback box set ([series help](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GMFKBUS43QQ5AJ5A)). KDP's [box set guidelines](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GD4TJFW4JEESLH4B) say it "encourages prioritizing individual titles linked to a series". They require a serialized release to say so in its description ("Episode 1," "Part 1 of 3") and to state that the work continues in later volumes. A box set of previously published titles should say so.

### P6. Pre-orders

**Verified primary.** Pre-orders are ebook-only and for new titles, with at most 10 active at a time. The final file is due 72 hours before release. A pre-order created on or after 2026-09-02 can be set up to 18 months out; earlier ones were limited to 12 months. [Pre-orders](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G201499380).

### P7. Updates reach existing KDP buyers

**Verified primary.** "For customers who bought your eBook before the update, the new version is delivered automatically after they finish a reading session and close the book," keeping their highlights and notes. Readers who have turned off Automatic Book Update don't receive it. KDP still declines *manual* update requests for added chapters or "significant changes that warrant a new edition", and the page doesn't say whether automatic delivery covers added chapters. [eBook update customer notifications](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200966010). Revisions to a KDP edition now mostly reach early buyers, but a large expansion may still belong in a new edition. Many older guides say the opposite; don't rely on them.

### P8. Print

**Verified primary.** KDP paperbacks need at least 24 pages, and spine text is printed only on books with "more than 79 pages". [Paperback submission guidelines](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G201857950). A short installment can technically be printed but makes a poor paperback, so in practice print means the collected edition. *Wool*'s print edition came only with the S&S deal (W10).

### P9. Leanpub royalties

**Verified primary.** "Leanpub's self-published authors earn 80% royalties on every sale of $7.99 and up, and 80% minus 50 cents on purchases below $7.99" ([About Leanpub](https://leanpub.com/about)). Since 2024 the minimum paid ebook price can be $0.99, or free; bundles start at $7.99 ([pricing article](https://help.leanpub.com/en/articles/10549058-leanpub-product-pricing-flexibility-to-enhance-your-book-marketing-opportunities-for-authors), 2025-02-13). A $0.99 sale earns about $0.29. Leanpub says it is "absolutely ok" to publish the same book on Leanpub and Amazon at once ([selling elsewhere](https://help.leanpub.com/en/articles/117414-can-i-sell-my-book-on-leanpub-and-on-amazon-or-on-other-self-publishing-platforms-at-the-same-time)), outside KDP Select (P4). The 2016 rate was 90% minus 50 cents; do not use it.

### P10. Gumroad and Payhip

**Verified primary.** Gumroad charges 10% + $0.50 per direct sale and 30% for sales through its Discover marketplace, with no monthly fee ([pricing](https://gumroad.com/pricing)). Payhip's free plan takes 5% per sale; its $29 and $99 monthly plans take 2% and 0%, and PayPal and Stripe fees apply on every plan ([pricing](https://payhip.com/pricing)).

### P11. Substack and Ghost

**Verified primary.** "Substack keeps 10% of subscription revenue, and Stripe charges its own payment processing fees" ([Substack support](https://support.substack.com/hc/en-us/articles/360037607131)). Ghost charges no transaction fee; its Starter plan is $18 a month and Publisher $29 a month, billed yearly ([Ghost pricing](https://ghost.org/pricing/)). Ghost's comparison table appears to limit paid subscriptions to Publisher and above; confirm before relying on it. Both sell access to posts. Whether either can sell a downloadable ebook was not checked.

### P12. Other stores

**Verified primary.** Kobo pays 70% on original works priced at $2.99 and up, with no cap, and 45% below ([Kobo Writing Life](https://kobowritinglife.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360058976032-What-will-my-earnings-be)). Apple Books pays "70% royalties on every ebook, regardless of price" ([Apple Books for Authors](https://authors.apple.com/)). Draft2Digital keeps about 10% of retail as commission, so authors get about 60% at most stores. It also charges a $20 activation fee, plus $12 a year for accounts earning under $100 ([D2D FAQ](https://draft2digital.com/faq/); [royalty rates](https://draft2digital.com/blog/royalty-rates/)).

### P13. Kindle Vella closed

**Verified primary for the closure; secondary for the December cutoff.** Amazon's FAQ says it wound down Kindle Vella "effective February 26, 2025" because it "didn't catch on as we'd hoped", and encourages authors to republish stories as ebooks ([Kindle Vella update FAQ](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GUJHP3574Z3DLPZD)). GeekWire reported the announcement on 2024-10-29 and relayed that no new episodes or tokens would be available after 2024-12-04 ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2024/amazon-to-shut-down-kindle-vella-serial-book-platform-saying-it-hasnt-caught-on-as-wed-hoped/)). No Amazon replacement for serialized reading was found.

## Part 4: Options for the primer

This section is an editorial assessment for Marshall to accept or reject. It assumes the marketing plan's sequence (free articles, then a self-published book, then a publisher pitch) and does not select a strategy.

| Model | Precedent | What's paid | Fit with free blog chapters | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. *Wool*-style KDP installments, then an omnibus | *Wool* (W2–W5), *Shift* (W8) | Each part at $0.99–$2.99, then the omnibus | Allowed outside Select (P3, P4) | Thin parts read as "too short" or duplicate the blog (P3); $0.99 earns about $0.35 (P2); each part must be labeled as part of a serial and can't point readers to the blog for the rest (P3, P5) |
| B. Leanpub in-progress book | Geerling (T2), Manning and PragProg betas (T3, T4) | One growing book, updated free for every buyer | Allowed; sell on KDP outside Select once complete (P9) | Smaller storefront than Amazon; Geerling's sales "exploded" once a complete edition was listed on Amazon (T2) |
| C. Free site, paid packaged book and early edition | Larson (T6), Weir (T10), Evans (T9) | The edited, convenient edition | The blog is the free site | Paid value has to come from editing, ordering and format, because the text is free |
| D. Paid newsletter early access | Beck (T7) | Subscription to drafts | Conflicts with the free-first principle in the marketing plan | Recurring obligation, and splits the audience between free and paid |

**Assessment.** *Wool* shows the loop to copy: a complete first installment, quick follow-ups chosen from reader response, a bundle for readers who wait, and an audience that becomes negotiating leverage. Its specific mechanism, paid KDP parts with a fixed text, fits the primer less well than its loop. Three facts push toward model B or C, or a mix of the two:

1. **The chapters will be free on the blog.** Readers will pay for the edited, ordered, offline edition, not for access to the words (T6, T9, T10). Thin paid installments that repeat free posts are the weakest product, and KDP's content rules work against them (P3).
2. **The subject changes fast.** Agent tooling will change during the writing. Leanpub is designed for a book that grows, delivering every revision to every buyer (T1). KDP now pushes revisions to earlier buyers too, but treats a large expansion as a possible new edition (P7). Kent Beck says the material for his follow-up book "was being overtaken by events" (T7).
3. **Posts and chapters drift apart.** Orosz planned to release book drafts as newsletter issues; the issues grew deeper and more timely than chapters, and he says it delayed the book by two years (T8). Larson avoided this by outlining the whole book first and writing each post as a chapter (T16). The primer's seven-part outline already exists, so the discipline is to write each article to fit its chapter slot, and let anything newsier become a separate post.

**One way to combine them, for discussion:** chapters go up free on yountlabs.com as articles. Each of the seven parts, once its chapters are written, is folded into a Leanpub in-progress edition, like Larson's paid early edition, at a low minimum price that rises as parts land. The early-edition releases serve as *Wool*'s installments: they set the cadence, gather feedback and build the buyer list. The complete edition then goes to KDP outside Select and to print. The publisher pitch has two precedents. Larson's chapters ran as blog posts before Stripe Press bought the finished manuscript (T16). Howey led with demonstrated sales and kept the e-book rights with a print-only deal (W10, W11). Either way, read the contract for terms on free copies: Crown had Weir take his free version down (T10), and Orosz feared Manning's terms could stop him sharing drafts (T8). Keeping the blog chapters up may need to be negotiated. The pitch rests on audience numbers this project doesn't have yet.

## Open questions for Marshall

1. Should paid editions follow each of the seven parts, or wait for the complete book?
2. Should anything be held back from the blog for the paid edition (exercises, worked examples, a part-level synthesis)? Larson kept only the foreword and ending off the site (T6). Some paid-only material strengthens model C, but it trades against the 90%-free principle in the marketing plan.
3. Is KDP Select's reach worth pulling chapters down to 10% after the book ships? This report assumes not, since the free articles are the marketing.
4. How often should installments come out? *Wool*'s four sequels arrived in about eight weeks (W4); no cadence has been selected for the primer.
